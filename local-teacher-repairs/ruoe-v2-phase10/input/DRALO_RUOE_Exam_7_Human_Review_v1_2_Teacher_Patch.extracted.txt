Text

The Human Skills That Still Matter

For years, people discussing future careers have focused on technical ability. Schools have promoted coding, companies have searched for data experts, and young people have been told that the safest path is to learn whatever machines cannot yet do. There is truth in that advice, but it leaves out an important point. In many workplaces, digital systems are no longer special tools used by a few experts; they are becoming part of everyday life. As a result, employers are paying at least as much attention to the skills that help people work with change, with information and with one another.

This does not mean that specialist knowledge has lost its value. A hospital still needs trained doctors, and a design firm still needs people who understand materials and production. Yet many managers now say that what separates strong employees from average ones is not simply what they know on the day they are hired. It is how quickly they can learn, unlearn and relearn. In industries where software is updated every few months, being confident enough to ask questions and adapt may matter more than having mastered one system that could soon disappear.

One skill that appears again and again in reports about future work is communication. This is sometimes misunderstood as meaning the ability to give polished presentations. In fact, employers are often thinking of something less dramatic and more useful: explaining ideas clearly, listening carefully, and adjusting your message for different audiences. A software engineer might need to describe a technical problem to a customer with no technical background. A scientist may have to persuade a local community that a project will benefit them. In both cases, success depends not on speaking more, but on making sure the other person understands.

Another ability that is rising in importance is judgement. Modern workers can reach huge amounts of information in seconds, and artificial intelligence can now produce summaries, plans and even images almost instantly. But fast access is not the same as wise use. People still need to decide whether a source is reliable, whether a suggestion fits the situation, and whether the result is fair. This is especially important in jobs that affect others directly, such as recruitment, education and healthcare. In these areas, a poor decision made at speed can create problems that take far longer to put right.

Teamwork is also changing. In the past, collaboration often meant sitting in the same room. Now it may involve colleagues spread across time zones, cultures and working styles. That creates opportunities, but it also increases the chance of misunderstanding. The most effective workers are therefore not simply friendly people who enjoy group tasks. They are people who can disagree without creating conflict, share responsibility fairly and keep a project moving when no one person is fully in control. These habits are harder to measure than exam results, but they shape whether work gets done well.

Some people worry that these so-called human skills are too vague to teach. However, they can be developed if education and training treat them as real abilities rather than personal qualities you either have or you do not. Students can practise giving feedback, solving open-ended problems and reflecting on mistakes. Early career employees can learn to lead small tasks before managing larger ones. The point is not that everyone must become endlessly flexible and cheerful. It is that future careers are likely to reward people who can combine practical knowledge with the maturity to use it well.

So the jobs of tomorrow may not belong only to those with the most advanced technical training. They may go to people who can keep learning, make sensible decisions and build trust with others while technology continues to change around them. In a labour market full of uncertainty, those abilities may turn out to be the most reliable expertise of all.

Text

The Less-You-Own Experiment

A few years ago, designer and podcast host Mia Turner realised that most of the things in her flat were not making life easier. She spent weekends moving piles of clothes to reach the wardrobe behind them, and kitchen drawers were so full that she avoided opening them. She was not trying to become a different person overnight; she simply wanted her home to feel calmer. (37) What surprised her most was not what disappeared, but how quickly daily routines became simpler once there was less to manage.

For many people, the idea of owning less is still linked to a rather severe image: white walls, one chair and a life free from personality. In practice, though, most people who reduce their belongings do not aim for emptiness. They are deciding what deserves space and attention. (38) A guitar that is played every evening, for example, earns its place far more clearly than a bread maker used once in three years.

Part of the appeal lies in the way possessions quietly demand our time. Every object must be cleaned, stored, repaired, charged, updated or eventually thrown away. Even gifts received with affection can become another decision to make. (39) Researchers who study consumer behaviour often point out that people do not only buy things; they also buy future responsibilities, though few notice that in the moment.

Social pressure, however, makes it difficult to step back. Shops and online adverts constantly suggest that a better version of ourselves is one purchase away: more organised, fitter, more creative or more relaxed. (40) If the trainers remain by the door unused, or the expensive notebook stays blank, the problem can feel personal rather than practical.

That is why many people begin with a small experiment rather than a dramatic clear-out. They choose one shelf, one drawer or one category, such as mugs or T-shirts, and question each item honestly. (41) Seeing immediate results in a limited area helps them build confidence before moving on to spaces with stronger emotional ties, such as books, gifts or childhood possessions.

None of this means that objects are unimportant. Some items connect us to family history, creative work or major moments in our lives, and reducing clutter does not require pretending otherwise. (42) When people are clear about that difference, their homes tend to reflect real priorities rather than old habits or delayed decisions.

Living with fewer possessions will not solve every modern problem, and most people who try it still enjoy buying useful or beautiful things. Yet many say the main reward is not a tidier shelf but a shift in attention. Instead of asking what else they need, they start noticing what already serves them well. In a culture that treats more as the default, that can be a surprisingly liberating change.

Texts

A – Leila Grant, bookseller

A few years ago, customers mainly came into my shop asking for gifts, while they bought most of their own reading online. Initially, I assumed that habit had changed for good. However, things have shifted again. Since the pandemic, people seem less interested in rushing through a screen all day and more willing to spend an evening with a novel. What surprises me most is that many younger customers ask staff for recommendations instead of relying on star ratings. They often tell me that reading feels like a form of rest rather than another task. I have also noticed people joining our monthly discussions even when they have not finished the book, simply because they want company. Sales matter, of course, yet what really convinces me something wider is happening is the atmosphere: the shop is noisier, slower and more social than it used to be.

B – Owen Price, engineer

I never thought of myself as a reader. At school I associated books with tests, and later I told myself I was too busy. Then, during a long train journey when my phone battery died, I started a paperback that had been sitting in my bag for months. By the time I arrived, I had forgotten to check messages. Since then, reading has become part of my commute. I still use technology all day for work, so opening a book feels refreshingly different. That said, I do not romanticise it. I choose crime fiction because it holds my attention, not because I want to become more cultured. Some friends have taken up reading to post impressive shelves online, which seems odd to me. For me, the appeal is simpler: books make dead time feel properly used, and they leave my head quieter than podcasts do.

C – Marta Silva, librarian

From where I work, the return to reading is real, but it is not quite the cosy story people imagine. Yes, our membership has risen steadily, and teenagers now borrow printed books as well as manga and audiobooks. Meanwhile, parents often say they are relieved to see their children choosing anything that is not a phone. Even so, I think the biggest reason is practical. Household budgets are tight, and libraries offer free entertainment, warmth and a reason to leave the house. Book clubs have filled up too, although many members admit they come as much for conversation as for literature. I do not see that as a weakness. In fact, shared reading can lead people towards more challenging authors later on. Still, I would be cautious about calling this a golden age. Enthusiasm is growing, yet people need access, time and quiet if it is to last.

D – Rafi Khan, presenter

When I started hosting a culture podcast, I expected film and music episodes to attract the biggest audiences. Strangely, the discussions about books are now downloaded far more. At first I assumed listeners wanted expert interviews, but the messages we receive suggest something else. People say they miss the concentration they once had, and hearing others talk enthusiastically about a title gives them the push to begin one themselves. I recognise that feeling. For years I bought serious books and left them unread beside my bed. Recently I stopped aiming to read what I thought I should read and chose shorter travel writing instead. That changed everything. Yet I am not convinced reading is returning because society has suddenly become calmer or wiser. If anything, people are seeking a way to resist constant distraction. Whether that habit survives depends on making room for it, not just admiring it from a distance.
