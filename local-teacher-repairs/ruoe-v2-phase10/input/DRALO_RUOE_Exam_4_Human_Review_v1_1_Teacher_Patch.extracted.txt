Part 5 — Multiple choice reading

Theme: Long-distance space travel

VISTA ALUMNO

Text

Living Beyond Earth

For decades, space travel was presented as a heroic adventure lasting a few thrilling days. Future missions are likely to be very different. A journey to Mars, for example, could take many months, with the crew living in a metal structure no bigger than a small flat. In that situation, success will depend not only on rockets and fuel, but also on whether people can stay healthy, motivated and cooperative when there is no quick way home.

That is why modern mission planning pays almost as much attention to ordinary daily life as to engineering. Researchers now study what astronauts eat, how they sleep and even how they argue. On the International Space Station, these questions matter, but support from Earth is always relatively close. For crews heading much farther away, however, every routine task becomes more important. If the coffee machine breaks in orbit around Earth, it is irritating; if the water recycling system fails halfway to Mars, it may threaten the whole mission.

Food offers a good example of this change in thinking. Early space meals were designed simply to be safe, light and easy to store. Future crews will still need practical food, but they may also need meals that provide comfort and variety. Some scientists are testing ways of growing salad leaves and herbs in small onboard gardens, not because astronauts cannot survive without fresh flavour, but because familiar smells and colours may improve mood. A handful of green plants might therefore do more than supply vitamins: it could remind the crew that life exists beyond plastic walls and computer screens.

Psychologists are equally interested in how groups behave over time. Films often show dramatic personal conflicts in space, yet real danger may come from quieter problems: boredom, tiredness and tiny habits that become unbearable after months together. In isolation studies on Earth, people have reported that the hardest part was not technical work but the lack of privacy and the feeling that every action was being observed. This suggests that future spacecraft will need not only excellent equipment but also carefully designed private corners where crew members can briefly escape one another.

Communication with Earth will also change. At great distances, messages can take many minutes to arrive, making ordinary conversation impossible. As a result, astronauts will have to solve more problems without immediate advice from mission control. This does not mean that experts on Earth will become unimportant. Rather, the balance of responsibility may shift. Space agencies have traditionally trained astronauts to follow detailed procedures, but future crews may need greater freedom to adapt when reality does not match the manual.

Even leisure, once treated as a minor issue, is becoming part of serious planning. Engineers have considered virtual windows, recorded natural sounds and digital libraries so that astronauts can choose how to relax. Critics sometimes dismiss such ideas as luxuries, arguing that every kilogram launched into space should have a clear practical use. Yet this view assumes that mental wellbeing is somehow separate from mission performance. In fact, a crew that can rest properly and recover from stress may be safer and more efficient than one supplied only with the bare essentials.

None of this guarantees that life on a long mission will be comfortable. Space will remain cramped, risky and deeply unfamiliar. But the image of astronauts as fearless machine operators is fading. The more experts learn about long-distance travel, the clearer it becomes that future missions will depend on human needs that are surprisingly down to Earth. In the end, reaching another planet may require us to think less about escaping ordinary life and more about how to carry the best parts of it with us.

Part 6 — Gapped text

Theme: Medical innovation

VISTA ALUMNO

Text

Small Breakthroughs, Big Changes

When people hear the phrase ‘medical innovation’, they often imagine dramatic scenes: a surgeon controlling a robot or scientists creating an entirely new organ in a laboratory. In reality, many of the advances that improve patients’ lives are less spectacular. They are often small changes to existing treatments, devices or systems, but they can still make an enormous difference. (37) For patients, that can mean less pain, shorter recovery times and more control over everyday life.

One area where this is clear is diabetes care. In the past, many people had to check their blood sugar by taking a drop of blood several times a day. Now, small sensors worn on the skin can measure levels continuously and send the information to a phone. (38) Instead of reacting to a problem after it has happened, users can often prevent it by eating, resting or adjusting medication sooner.

Another important change has come in hearing technology. Traditional hearing aids were useful, but many users complained that they amplified every sound equally, making busy places exhausting. Newer devices can separate speech from background noise far more effectively. They can also connect directly to phones, televisions and public sound systems. (39) As a result, many people feel more confident about going to restaurants, attending meetings or simply chatting in a crowded room.

Not all innovations are pieces of equipment. Some involve rethinking how care is delivered. In several countries, hospitals now allow certain patients to recover at home while being monitored remotely. Nurses visit when necessary, and medical staff review temperature, heart rate and other readings online. (40) For many patients, sleeping in their own bed and staying close to family can be as valuable as the treatment itself.

3D printing is also beginning to change medicine in practical ways. It is already used to produce detailed models of a patient’s body before complex operations. Surgeons can study these models in advance and plan difficult procedures more carefully. (41) This matters because a slightly better fit can reduce discomfort and encourage people to keep using the item they need.

Perhaps the most striking progress, however, is in rehabilitation. People who have lost movement after an injury or stroke can now practise with robotic supports that guide their arms or legs through repeated actions. The machines do not replace therapists, but they allow patients to perform more precise movements more often. (42) Even when recovery is slow, noticing these small gains can be highly motivating.

Of course, not every promising idea becomes a success. New products can be expensive, and some are difficult to make widely available. Doctors and patients also need time to learn how to use them properly. Even so, the direction is encouraging. Medical progress is not only about discovering cures; it is also about making daily life safer, easier and more independent for millions of people.

A – Liam Porter

I used to book flights without thinking twice, mainly because my work involved short notice meetings in different cities. Initially, I saw trains as the slower option for people with time to spare. That changed after a winter storm left me sleeping on an airport bench while the rail services were mostly back to normal by morning. Since then, I still fly for some journeys abroad, yet for trips within the country I nearly always choose the train. I can open my laptop, answer emails and arrive feeling as if the day has already started, rather than as if I have survived an obstacle course. It is usually pricier than the budget flights my colleagues hunt down, although once I add airport transfers and parking, the gap shrinks. For me, the calmer experience matters as much as the timetable.

B – Sonia Malik

My choice between rail and air is largely practical rather than ideological. I live in a town with a small airport twenty minutes away, while the nearest mainline station is a bus ride and a long wait from my front door. Because of that, flying often wins for family visits, especially when I am travelling with two energetic children and several bags. People assume airports are stressful, but I find them manageable if I check in online and keep expectations realistic. However, I would never claim planes are always better. Last summer, after a delayed departure and a missed connection, I spent more than twelve hours getting to somewhere that is only six by train. Meanwhile, the children were exhausted. If a direct rail service existed from where I live, I suspect I would switch immediately for many journeys.

C – Harriet Doyle

I am one of those people who genuinely enjoy the journey itself, so trains suit me perfectly. Looking out of the window helps me settle into a trip in a way flying never has. At airports, everything seems designed to hurry you from one queue to another, and I arrive already irritated. By contrast, on trains I read, daydream or chat to whoever is beside me. That said, I am not romantic about it. I have had cramped seats, broken air conditioning and one memorable evening when we stopped in open countryside for two hours with no explanation. Even so, I remain loyal to rail because it feels connected to real places instead of sealed off from them. Friends tell me I should take cheaper flights for weekend breaks, yet I would rather spend longer travelling than give up that sense of gradual arrival.

D – Noah Greene

Until recently, I had hardly travelled by train beyond my daily commute. I assumed long-distance rail would be unreliable and old-fashioned, so for years I booked flights automatically, especially for city breaks in Europe. Then my partner persuaded me to try an overnight train to the continent instead of flying. Frankly, I expected to regret it. Yet the trip turned out to be the highlight of the holiday: we boarded in the evening, had dinner, slept surprisingly well and woke up in the centre of our destination. Since then, I have become far more interested in planning routes that avoid airports altogether, although I know this approach only works if you organise well in advance. The one drawback is that rail journeys sometimes demand more research and flexibility. Even so, I now see travel time as part of the experience rather than wasted hours to be cut.
