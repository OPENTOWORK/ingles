Part 5 — Multiple-choice reading

Theme: Turning a hobby into a career

VISTA ALUMNO

Read the text and choose the answer (A, B, C or D) which you think fits best according to the text.

Text

When the Weekend Job Becomes Your Real One

People often say, half-jokingly, that they would happily do their hobby for a living if only someone would pay them. It sounds like a fantasy: spending each day baking, repairing bicycles, taking photographs or training dogs instead of answering emails in an office. Yet for a growing number of people, this is not simply a daydream. Online platforms, specialist markets and social media have made it easier than ever to reach customers directly. At the same time, the idea of a single career for life has weakened, and many workers now expect to change direction at least once.

Even so, the move from hobby to profession is rarely as simple as outsiders imagine. What begins as a relaxing escape from daily pressures can quickly acquire deadlines, invoices and awkward customer requests. The ceramic artist who once enjoyed shaping bowls at midnight whenever inspiration appeared may suddenly need to produce thirty matching mugs by Friday. A guitarist who loved experimenting alone may find that teaching beginners pays the bills better than performing does. In other words, earning money from a hobby does not just add income; it changes the activity itself.

That shift can be uncomfortable, especially at the start. Some new business owners discover that the part they most enjoyed is now surrounded by tasks they had never considered. Selling handmade soap, for instance, may involve packaging rules, photography, stock records and constant communication with buyers. There is also the emotional difficulty of attaching a price to something personal. Charging friends for portraits or asking strangers to pay for cakes based on family recipes can feel less like business and more like self-exposure. For that reason, people who succeed are not always the most talented. Often, they are the ones who can tolerate uncertainty and keep going while they learn unfamiliar skills.

Financially, caution matters. Stories about overnight success attract attention, but they hide the slower reality. Many people begin by working evenings and weekends, testing whether customers will return before leaving a secure salary. That approach can be exhausting, but it provides useful information. It reveals whether the product is genuinely wanted, whether prices cover costs and whether enthusiasm survives repetition. A person may love making jewellery, for example, yet feel very differently after producing the same popular design fifty times in one month. Better to discover that while the hobby is still optional than after rent depends on it.

There is another less obvious challenge: identity. When people ask what you do, saying "I'm trying to make a living from restoring old furniture" can sound uncertain even if the business is developing well. Society still tends to respect conventional job titles more than self-created ones, particularly during the messy middle stage when income is irregular. Family members may worry that a sensible career is being thrown away, while former colleagues assume the change is temporary. Such reactions can make people doubt themselves, not because the new path is wrong, but because it has fewer recognised milestones.

None of this means turning a hobby into a career is a mistake. For some, it leads to a more satisfying working life precisely because it combines personal interest with independence. But the most successful people are usually those who treat passion as a starting point, not a guarantee. They accept that professionalising a hobby means protecting what they love while also submitting it to market forces. The reward is not endless enjoyment untouched by pressure. It is the chance to build work that feels meaningful, provided they are willing to see the hobby clearly, not romantically.

Text

Beyond the Usual Game

For years, unusual sports were often treated as little more than internet curiosities: fun to watch for a minute, but too strange to be taken seriously. That view is changing quickly. Across Europe and beyond, activities once known only to small groups of enthusiasts are attracting larger crowds, more media attention and a steady flow of newcomers. (37) As a result, sports such as underwater hockey, bike polo and ultimate frisbee are no longer seen as passing trends.

Part of the appeal is that these sports offer people a fresh experience in a world where many leisure activities already feel heavily organised and familiar. New players are often surprised by how welcoming the atmosphere can be. In mainstream sports, beginners may feel pressure to perform well immediately, especially if teams are focused on results. (38) That makes it easier for people to try something without worrying that they will be judged.

The growth of social media has also played an important role. Twenty years ago, a local group might have struggled to find enough members to survive. Today, one short video of a fast-moving match can reach thousands of viewers in a few hours, and many of them will share it with friends. (39) In other words, online platforms do not simply advertise these sports; they help create the communities that keep them alive.

Another reason for their popularity is practical rather than cultural. Some unusual sports are easier to organise than people expect, especially in cities where space is limited. Bike polo, for example, can be played on a hard court that might otherwise sit empty in the evening. (40) For adults with busy schedules, that flexibility can make the difference between taking part regularly and giving up after a few weeks.

Even so, becoming more visible brings difficulties. Once a sport begins to attract sponsorship, formal competitions and governing bodies, arguments often follow about what should happen next. Some players welcome clearer rules and better funding, saying these changes make the sport safer and more sustainable. Others worry that the original spirit will be lost. (41) What began as an informal activity can start to resemble the very sports it once offered an alternative to.

This tension is especially clear when schools and universities become involved. On the one hand, official recognition can provide equipment, coaching and reliable places to train. It can also introduce young people to sports they might never otherwise discover. (42) If every session is turned into preparation for a tournament, there is less room for the creativity and experimentation that attracted many early participants in the first place.

For now, though, the rise of unusual sports seems likely to continue. People are searching for activities that combine exercise with friendship, challenge and a sense of discovery. Whether they remain proudly unconventional or slowly become part of the sporting mainstream, they are already changing ideas about what a sport can look like. That may be the most interesting development of all.

Texts

A – Rosa Mendez

I used to laugh at people who checked their phones every few minutes, yet now I rely on a smartwatch more than I expected. I bought it initially because I kept missing messages while cycling across town to my bakery. Since then, though, it has quietly changed my routine in other ways. The reminders to stand up or drink water are useful, and paying for ingredients with a tap feels quicker than searching for coins with flour on my hands. However, I switched off most notifications after a few weeks. I realised I was letting tiny vibrations interrupt conversations with customers. My brother says these devices make life easier only if you control them, and I think he is right. I still enjoy the health tracking, but I refuse to wear the watch in the evening. Home should feel different from work.

B – Gareth Cole

For me, smart devices became essential during a long recovery after a knee operation. Before that, I had seen them as expensive toys. A friend persuaded me to try a voice-controlled speaker and a sleep monitor, and I was surprised by how much independence they gave me. From bed, I could adjust the lights, listen to the news and even make shopping lists without asking for help. Meanwhile, the sleep data helped me notice a pattern I would otherwise have ignored: pain medication was affecting my rest. That said, I am uneasy about how much personal information these gadgets collect. I read every privacy setting carefully, although I know most people never bother. Even so, I have kept the devices because they solved practical problems at a time when I really needed support, and they still save me effort now.

C – Leila Hassan

As a secondary school teacher, I spend all day asking teenagers to put their phones away, so friends find it amusing that my flat is full of connected devices. Still, I am selective about what deserves space in my life. I love my smart thermostat because it learns when I am usually home and stops me wasting money on heating. By contrast, I returned a robot vacuum after a month. It was entertaining at first, yet it constantly got stuck under a chair and demanded more attention than an ordinary broom. What annoys me most is the assumption that newer always means better. Sometimes the simplest option is still the most sensible one. Even so, I admit that when my train is delayed and my lights turn on automatically before I arrive, it feels oddly reassuring rather than unnecessary.

D – Noah Price

I review consumer technology for a local website, so people expect me to fill my house with the latest devices. Actually, I try to live with only a few, because testing too many products has made me less easily impressed. At first, every new feature seems clever; later, you notice how many are solutions to problems nobody had. The exception for me is a tiny tracker attached to my keys and wallet. I travel constantly for work, and before buying it I wasted ridiculous amounts of time searching hotel rooms and train seats. Now I can locate things in seconds, which lowers my stress more than any fancy entertainment system could. Yet I never recommend buying smart devices simply because they are fashionable. If a product does not remove a real frustration from your day, the excitement usually disappears fast.
