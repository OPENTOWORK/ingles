Text

The Unexpected Value of Volunteering

When students think about improving their job prospects, they often imagine expensive courses, polished CVs and hours spent online searching for internships. Volunteering rarely appears at the top of the list. It is still seen by some as something generous but separate from professional life, a pleasant extra for people with spare time. Yet employers are increasingly interested in applicants who have chosen to work without pay, not because unpaid work is automatically impressive, but because it can reveal qualities that are difficult to teach in a classroom.

One reason is that volunteering places people in situations where they must act rather than simply discuss. A university seminar may explore teamwork in theory, but helping to organise a local sports event or supporting staff in a charity shop demands cooperation in real time. Volunteers often have to solve small problems quickly, speak to strangers and deal with the unexpected. None of this turns someone instantly into the ideal employee, of course. However, it does provide evidence that a person can cope when instructions are incomplete and conditions are less than perfect.

This practical side matters because many employers are cautious about grades on their own. A strong academic record suggests intelligence and determination, but not necessarily reliability or initiative. Managers know that a new recruit may understand the theory of customer service and still struggle when faced with an impatient customer or a busy office. Volunteering can help close that gap. A candidate who has spent Saturday mornings coordinating food-bank deliveries or running activities for children is able to point to specific experience rather than offering vague claims about being organised and good with people.

There is another, less obvious advantage. Volunteering can widen a person's view of work itself. Someone helping at an environmental project may discover that they enjoy planning tasks more than carrying them out, while a volunteer role in a museum might lead to an interest in communications rather than history. In this sense, unpaid work is not just useful for getting a job; it can also prevent people from chasing the wrong one. Career advisers often talk about finding your strengths, but many people only recognise them when they are doing something that feels meaningful.

That said, volunteering is not a magic key that opens every door. Employers can usually tell when applicants mention it simply because they think they should. Listing a single afternoon spent planting trees will not transform a weak application, and pretending to care about a cause is risky in interview, where enthusiasm is difficult to fake for long. The real benefit comes when people reflect on what they learned. Even routine tasks can become valuable examples if applicants explain clearly how they developed patience, confidence or judgement.

For this reason, students should perhaps think less about which volunteer role will look most impressive and more about which one they are likely to take seriously. The best experiences are often local and ordinary: helping at a youth club, assisting at a community festival or supporting an elderly neighbour with shopping and technology. Such activities may sound modest, but they involve trust, consistency and contact with others. In a competitive job market, those qualities can matter as much as technical knowledge. Volunteering will not replace qualifications, but it can give them a more human shape.

Text

What the Sea Keeps Hidden

For centuries, the deep sea was imagined as an empty and rather lifeless place. Divers could only reach shallow water, and early maps showed huge areas of ocean floor as blank spaces. That picture has changed dramatically in the last few decades, as better submarines, cameras and sensors have allowed researchers to explore further than ever before. (37) As a result, the seabed is now seen not as a flat desert, but as a varied landscape full of life, movement and surprising discoveries.

One of the most dramatic moments came when scientists first found hydrothermal vents in the Pacific Ocean. These are cracks in the seabed where extremely hot water, rich in minerals, pours out from below the Earth's surface. (38) Yet around these dark openings, researchers discovered thick communities of tube worms, crabs and bacteria that were thriving without any sunlight at all.

That finding forced biologists to rethink one of their most basic assumptions. Until then, nearly all known ecosystems were thought to depend, directly or indirectly, on energy from the Sun. In vent communities, however, bacteria gain energy from chemicals released by the Earth itself, and other creatures feed on them. (39) It also raised new questions about whether similar forms of life might exist on other planets or moons with oceans beneath ice.

Discoveries beneath the sea are not limited to living things. Shipwrecks, tools and even entire lost settlements have been found underwater, preserved in mud or cold, dark conditions. Marine archaeologists say the sea can act like a giant storage room, protecting objects that would quickly rot or break apart on land. (40) In some cases, a wreck does more than reveal a single disaster: it can offer a detailed picture of trade, diet and daily routine in a past society.

Modern technology has made this kind of work far more precise. Remotely operated vehicles can stay underwater for hours, collecting samples and sending back high-quality images. Powerful scanning systems can also build detailed maps of the seabed before anyone decides where to explore more closely. (41) Instead of relying on chance, research teams can now compare data, identify unusual shapes and return to exact spots again and again.

However, greater access brings new problems. Deep-sea tourism, fishing and plans for underwater mining are expanding into places that were once difficult to reach. Scientists worry that habitats could be damaged before they are properly understood, especially because many deep-sea species grow slowly and may take decades to recover. (42) For that reason, several research groups are calling for stronger international rules to protect vulnerable areas before commercial activity increases.

The sea still covers more than two thirds of the planet, and much of it remains unexplored. Each expedition adds a few more details to a picture that is still incomplete. What lies below is not simply a collection of strange creatures or sunken objects, but a record of how Earth works and how people have lived on it. The more we discover, the clearer it becomes that the ocean floor is one of the world's last great frontiers.

A – Leah Morton, junior doctor

During medical school, I treated sleep as optional. If I had an early placement, I stayed up revising and assumed coffee would solve everything. Initially, that seemed true, yet once I started work in a hospital, the effect of poor sleep became impossible to ignore. I wasn't just tired; I was slower, less patient and far too likely to forget small details. One night shift, I wrote down a dosage and then immediately checked it again because I no longer trusted my own concentration. Since then, I've become quite protective of my routine. I still can't control every shift pattern, of course, but I prepare for busy weeks by going to bed earlier and keeping my phone out of reach. Friends joke that I've become boring. However, feeling alert at work matters more to me than late-night messages or another episode of a series.

B – Noah Price, university rowing coach

People assume my athletes need speeches about discipline, whereas what they often need is permission to rest. A few years ago, I used to praise anyone who trained before dawn and studied late into the evening. Meanwhile, several of them were getting ill, arguing with teammates and performing worse in races. It took one frank conversation with our team physiotherapist to make me reconsider. She pointed out that we were planning recovery sessions carefully but ignoring the simplest kind of recovery there is. Since then, I ask students about their sleeping habits as seriously as I ask about nutrition. I still admire determination, although I'm no longer impressed by people who boast that they manage on four or five hours. In my experience, that attitude sounds tough but usually leads to poor decisions, especially when pressure builds before a competition.

C – Priya Shah, software designer

For years, I believed my best ideas arrived after midnight. My flat was quiet then, emails stopped, and I enjoyed the feeling that everyone else had switched off while I was still creating. The problem was that mornings became a blur. I worked from home, so I could hide it for a while, yet during online meetings I sometimes missed obvious points or sounded uninterested when I was simply exhausted. Last winter, I agreed to join a colleague for an early walk before work. I expected it to last a week; instead, it changed my routine completely. I now shut my laptop at a sensible time, and although I occasionally lose an evening of work, I gain much clearer thinking the next day. Strangely, better sleep hasn't made me less imaginative. If anything, it has helped me turn ideas into plans I can actually follow.

D – Gareth Ellis, long-distance lorry driver

I've spent most of my working life on the road, so I know exactly how tempting it is to keep going when you're only an hour from home. Earlier in my career, I did that far too often. I told myself opening the window or turning up the radio was enough. It wasn't. Nothing serious happened, thankfully, but there were moments when the motorway signs seemed to appear out of nowhere, which frightened me more than I admitted at the time. Nowadays I plan breaks before I set off and use service stations without feeling guilty about lost time. Some drivers still see sleep as something that gets in the way of earning money. Yet I think that's short-sighted. Being awake for long stretches doesn't prove commitment; it simply increases the chance of mistakes. My family certainly prefers the calmer version of me that arrives home after proper rest.
