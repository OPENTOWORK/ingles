Part 5 — Multiple-choice reading

Theme: Impulse buying psychology

VISTA ALUMNO

Read the text and choose the answer (A, B, C or D) which you think fits best according to the text.

Text

The Things We Never Planned to Buy

Most of us know the feeling. We go into a shop for toothpaste and come out carrying a candle, a notebook and a packet of biscuits as well. We click online to replace a phone charger and somehow end up paying for trainers we had not even considered a minute earlier. Later, when the excitement has gone, we may wonder why we wanted any of it. Yet this pattern is so common that businesses spend huge amounts trying to understand it. Their success does not depend only on making useful products. It also relies on recognising the small psychological nudges that can turn a passing glance into a purchase.

One of the strongest nudges is the hope that an object might improve not just our lives but our identities. People do not only buy running shoes because they need something on their feet. They may also be buying a picture of themselves as healthier, busier or more disciplined. The same thing happens with kitchen equipment, expensive pens and travel bags. A product can seem to promise a future version of us, and that future can be more attractive than the object itself. This helps explain why unopened items often gather dust in cupboards: they were bought for the person we meant to become, not the one we already were.

Shops and websites are skilled at making that imagined future feel close. Online stores, for example, recommend items by suggesting that people similar to us chose them, too. This can be useful, but it also removes some of the pause in which we might ask whether we need the item at all. Physical shops do something similar through layout. Essentials are rarely near the entrance, so customers travel past rows of things they never intended to see. None of this forces anyone to buy. But the environment quietly increases the number of moments in which buying feels easy and saying no feels like an unnecessary effort.

Another reason we overspend is that modern shopping often separates the pleasure of choosing from the pain of paying. Cash once made the cost visible and immediate. Now many purchases happen with a quick tap, and online payments may be completed before we have properly noticed the total. Subscription services push this even further. A small monthly charge can feel harmless, even if several of them together cost far more than a single larger payment would. People are not foolish for missing this. Our brains tend to notice immediate rewards more clearly than slow, repeated losses.

Emotions matter just as much. Buying can create a brief sense of control, comfort or reward, especially after a difficult day. That is why boredom, stress and loneliness are such powerful shopping partners. The item itself may be almost irrelevant; what matters is the lift that comes from deciding, receiving and opening. Companies are well aware of this and often design the process to feel smooth and satisfying, from attractive packaging to messages announcing that an order is on its way. These details may appear minor, but together they turn shopping into an experience rather than a simple exchange.

None of this means that people are helpless or that all unplanned buying is a mistake. Pleasure has value, and an unexpected purchase can sometimes be a good one. The problem starts when buying becomes our automatic response to every mood or every clever suggestion. The most effective solution is not usually strict self-denial. It is to create enough delay for thought to return: leaving an online basket overnight, unsubscribing from marketing emails, or deciding in advance what kind of shopper we want to be. The aim is not to remove enjoyment from spending, but to make sure our money follows our decisions instead of somebody else's design.

Theme: Healthy eating on a budget

VISTA ALUMNO

Six sentences have been removed from the article. Choose from the sentences A–G the one which fits each gap. There is one extra sentence which you do not need to use.

Text

Eating Well for Less

For many people, healthy eating seems to belong in the same category as luxury gym memberships and expensive kitchen gadgets. Fresh ingredients are often seen as something for shoppers with plenty of time and money. Yet nutrition experts repeatedly point out that eating well does not depend on buying the most fashionable products in the supermarket. (37) Once that idea changes, planning cheaper and healthier meals becomes much more realistic.

One reason healthy food appears costly is that people compare individual items rather than whole meals. A ready-made pizza may look cheaper than buying vegetables, rice and chicken separately. However, the pizza usually feeds fewer people and leaves nothing for the next day. (38) In other words, the better comparison is not product against product, but meal against meal.

Planning is one of the simplest ways to control food spending. People who decide what they will cook before shopping are less likely to buy snacks, duplicate ingredients or food they never use. A short list also helps shoppers ignore special offers that are not really useful. (39) That way, ingredients bought for one dish can often be used in another later in the week.

Some of the best-value foods are ones many shoppers walk past without much thought. Dried lentils, oats, eggs, frozen peas and tinned tomatoes are rarely advertised as exciting, but they are versatile and nutritious. They can become soups, pasta sauces, curries or quick lunches with very little effort. (40) Choosing a few reliable basics like these can reduce both cost and stress.

Another useful habit is being flexible about where nutrition comes from. Many people build meals around meat and then add vegetables to the side. That can work, but it is often the most expensive approach. Beans, chickpeas and eggs provide protein at a much lower price, and they can still produce filling meals. (41) The point is not to give up meat completely, but to use it more carefully.

Of course, even careful shoppers waste money if food ends up in the bin. Leftovers are not exciting, which is why they are often forgotten until it is too late. But a small amount of cooked rice, roast vegetables or chicken can easily become lunch the next day. (42) Besides saving money, this habit also saves the time and energy needed to cook from the beginning again.

In the end, healthy eating on a budget is less about finding one perfect shopping trick and more about building several sensible habits. None of them is especially complicated: compare meals, plan ahead, keep basic ingredients at home and use what you buy. Taken together, those choices can make home cooking cheaper than many people expect. They also show that eating well is not reserved for people with unusually large food budgets.

F A large pot of soup or curry, for example, can provide several portions at a reasonable cost.

Theme: Tourism and local communities

Texts

A – Luca Ferris, café owner

When the first budget flights began using the airport nearby, our town changed almost overnight. Initially, I was delighted: I run a small café on the harbour, and extra visitors meant I could stay open all year instead of closing every winter. Since then, however, the success has had awkward side effects. Rents have risen so sharply that two of my staff now share a flat an hour away. I also miss recognising faces in the street. Yet I would be dishonest if I pretended tourism had brought nothing but problems. The council finally repaired the seafront because holidaymakers complained online, and local fishermen now sell directly to restaurants rather than through middlemen. What bothers me most is not the number of tourists, but the way decisions are made without asking those of us who live and work here.

B – Hannah Price, volunteer guide

I moved to the village ten years ago for a quieter life, so I was sceptical when coaches started arriving every weekend. Meanwhile, the old mill, which had stood empty for years, was turned into a visitor centre, and I agreed to help there one afternoon a week. To my surprise, meeting travellers has made me appreciate the place more, because their questions force me to notice details I had ignored. Even so, I worry that some businesses now present a polished version of local life that residents barely recognise. The summer craft market, for instance, used to be where neighbours swapped homemade goods; now it can feel like a performance. Still, I would rather manage tourism carefully than try to stop it. People will keep coming, and if locals take part, we have a better chance of protecting what matters.

C – Sanjay Mehta, secondary school teacher

Our mountain region has long depended on farming, but poor harvests pushed many families into tourist work. At first, I welcomed that shift because several parents finally had a stable income. Yet after a few seasons, I noticed something less obvious in school. Teenagers were leaving classes early to help in guesthouses, especially during peak months, and some no longer saw education as worthwhile. There is also pressure to learn only the kind of English needed for serving visitors, which seems far too narrow to me. On the other hand, tourism has funded a new sports hall that the whole community uses, and a few former students have returned after university to start walking tours with real respect for local traditions. So I am not against visitors; I simply think short-term earnings should not decide the region’s future.

D – Marta Zielińska, town councillor

By the time I joined the council, residents were already angry about crowded buses and noisy holiday flats, so any proposal linked to tourism was bound to cause arguments. I understood the frustration, yet I also knew the town could not rely on its declining fishing industry forever. We introduced limits on short-term rentals and changed rubbish collection times, which helped a little. What surprised me was how quickly tempers cooled once people saw that complaints led to action. Even now, though, I am uneasy when campaigners talk as if tourism itself were the enemy. In my view, the real issue is whether profits stay locally or disappear into companies based elsewhere. If more small firms were owned by residents, the conversation would be far less bitter. Since then, I have become convinced that control matters more than growth.
