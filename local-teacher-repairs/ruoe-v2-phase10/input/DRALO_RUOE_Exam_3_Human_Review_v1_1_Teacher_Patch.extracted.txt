Part 5 — Multiple-choice reading

Theme: Everyday fitness

VISTA ALUMNO

Read the text and choose the answer (A, B, C or D) which you think fits best according to the text.

Text

Fitness That Fits Real Life

Many people decide to get fitter in the same dramatic way: they buy expensive trainers, join a gym and promise themselves they will go five times a week. Two weeks later, the trainers are by the door, the gym card is in a drawer and the plan has quietly disappeared. This cycle is so common that it is easy to believe staying fit must involve a huge effort. In fact, the opposite is often true. The routines that last are usually the ones that fit into daily life so neatly that they barely feel like routines at all.

One reason people give up is that they choose exercise that looks impressive rather than exercise they will actually do. A friend may be training for a half marathon, and social media is full of intense workout videos, but that does not mean running long distances or lifting heavy weights is the right starting point for everyone. If your first plan already feels like punishment, you are unlikely to repeat it. Walking to work, cycling to the shops or doing a short online class in the living room may sound less exciting, but they are far easier to keep doing when work gets busy or the weather changes.

This does not mean easy exercise has little value. In recent years, health experts have become much more interested in ordinary movement spread across the day. Climbing stairs, carrying shopping bags and standing up regularly from a desk can all make a difference, especially for people who spend hours sitting down. Ten minutes here and there may not look important in isolation, yet the effect adds up. The point is not that a brisk walk is equal to a serious training session, but that people often ignore useful activity because it does not match their image of what exercise should look like.

There is also a psychological advantage in keeping things simple. Large fitness goals can be inspiring, but they can also create an uncomfortable feeling of failure before you have properly begun. If the target is to become a different person by next month, any missed session seems to prove you were never committed. Smaller goals work differently. Promising yourself a short walk after dinner or a few stretches before breakfast makes success more likely, and success matters because it changes identity. Once people start seeing themselves as someone who moves regularly, they often become more willing to do a little more.

Of course, convenience should not be confused with carelessness. People sometimes use the idea of "keeping active" to avoid anything that raises the heart rate or requires planning. Fetching a cup of tea from the kitchen is movement, but it is not enough on its own. The most helpful approach is probably a mixture: build more activity into ordinary life, then protect a small number of moments each week for something slightly harder. That could mean a swim on Saturday, a longer bike ride or a dance class with a friend. The details matter less than the fact that the habit is realistic.

Perhaps the most useful change, then, is not in the body but in the story people tell themselves about fitness. If exercise is treated as a test of discipline, many will delay starting until they have the right clothes, enough time or stronger motivation. If it is seen instead as a practical part of everyday life, the barrier becomes lower. You do not have to wait for a perfect Monday or a burst of enthusiasm. You can begin with the journey to work, the stairs at the station or ten minutes before bed, and let consistency do the rest.

Text

Always Connected?

A generation ago, staying in touch usually meant planning ahead. People arranged a time to call, wrote letters that took days to arrive, or waited until they met face to face. Today, a message can cross the world in seconds, and that speed has changed not only how often we communicate but also what we expect from one another. Friends, relatives and colleagues can now reach us almost whenever they want, wherever we happen to be.

For many people, the biggest change is the disappearance of distance as a real obstacle. Video calls allow grandparents to read bedtime stories from another country, while work teams can discuss a project without sharing an office. (37) As a result, people often maintain relationships that might once have become weaker over time. Regular contact no longer depends on living in the same place, or even in the same time zone.

Another major shift is the movement from delayed replies to instant reactions. In the past, silence usually had a practical explanation: a person was out, a letter had not arrived, or a phone line was busy. Now, when someone sees a message and does not answer, others may read meaning into that pause. (38) Technology has therefore created a new form of social pressure, in which speed itself can seem like a sign of care, interest or respect.

At the same time, digital communication has changed the style of everyday language. Emails were once seen as quite formal, but messaging apps have encouraged shorter sentences, quicker exchanges and a more relaxed tone. (39) Although some critics worry that this makes writing less careful, others point out that people are actually writing far more often than previous generations did. Teenagers who rarely send letters may still produce thousands of words a week on their phones.

The rise of group chats has brought its own advantages and problems. Families use them to share news, sports teams organise lifts there, and groups of friends make plans without sending ten separate messages. (40) Yet these spaces can also be exhausting. Conversations continue all day, jokes arrive too quickly for everyone to follow, and some members feel obliged to react even when they have nothing useful to add.

Public communication has changed as well. Social media allows individuals, companies and public figures to speak directly to large audiences without relying on traditional newspapers or broadcasters. (41) A funny post, a short video or a personal comment can reach millions within hours. This has made communication more democratic in some ways, but it has also made mistakes more visible and harder to control once they begin to spread.

Despite all these developments, older forms of communication have not completely disappeared. Important conversations are still often saved for face-to-face meetings, especially when emotions are involved or decisions are difficult. (42) In the end, technology has not replaced human communication; it has expanded it. We now have more ways to connect than ever before, but choosing the right one has become a skill in itself.

Texts

A – Leah Morgan

At first, I thought trying to live more sustainably meant making huge sacrifices, so I kept putting it off. Then my electricity bill rose sharply, and that finally pushed me to act. I started with ordinary habits: switching devices off properly, washing clothes at lower temperatures and carrying a flask instead of buying takeaway coffee. None of it felt dramatic, yet after a few months the changes became routine. Meanwhile, my neighbours set up a chat group where we exchange tips, and that has helped me stick to my plans. I still fly to visit my brother once a year, so I would never claim to be perfect. However, I have stopped worrying about getting every decision right. For me, the biggest lesson is that practical changes matter more than guilt, especially when they save money as well.

B – Nikhil Patel

My interest in climate action began for a different reason. I work as a chef, so I see every day how much food gets thrown away in homes and restaurants. Initially, I focused on composting and planning meals better, but since then I have become more ambitious. These days, I cycle to work most mornings, even though the traffic can be unpleasant, and I have cut down sharply on meat. Friends sometimes assume I enjoy setting strict rules for myself, yet that is not really true. In fact, I get annoyed when people turn small changes into a competition about who is the greenest. Even so, I do think convenience is often just an excuse. Once I learnt a few basic recipes and started using leftovers creatively, wasting less food turned out to be easier than I had expected.

C – Sophie Green

I came to everyday climate action through my children. They returned from school full of ideas and immediately questioned why our family drove such short distances. To be honest, I resisted at first because my timetable already felt impossible. Since then, though, walking more and borrowing things instead of buying them have changed how I think about daily life. What surprised me most was the social side of it. Our street now runs occasional repair evenings, and people who barely spoke before now share tools and fix lamps, zips and bikes together. That sense of community matters to me as much as the environmental benefit. I am still doubtful about some trends online, especially expensive products marketed as essential for a greener lifestyle. If an action creates pressure to spend more, I usually question whether it is really the best solution.

D – Owen Kerr

For years I read articles about the climate and agreed with them in theory, yet I carried on living exactly as before. What changed was joining a local river-cleaning day. It was only a Saturday morning, but seeing piles of litter beside water I had known since childhood made the issue feel immediate rather than abstract. Since then, I have tried to reduce packaging, use public transport more often and buy fewer new clothes. I am not naturally organised, so I need systems: a shopping list, a reusable bag kept by the door, and reminders on my phone. Without those, I slip back into old habits surprisingly fast. I also think some campaigns ask too much from individuals while ignoring larger companies. Even so, taking action myself has stopped me feeling powerless, which is why I encourage other people to begin somewhere, however small.
