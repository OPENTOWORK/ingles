Theme: Driverless transport

VISTA ALUMNO

Read the text and choose the answer (A, B, C or D) which you think fits best according to the text.

Text

When the Steering Wheel Lets Go

For years, images of driverless transport belonged to science fiction: silent vehicles gliding through cities while passengers read, sleep or work. Recently, however, that future has begun to look less distant. In several places, buses already follow fixed routes without a human driver, and robot taxis are being tested on public roads. Yet if you ask transport engineers when driverless travel will become normal, many give a careful answer. The technology has advanced quickly, they say, but the bigger challenge is fitting it into the untidy reality of everyday life.

A machine can be trained to recognise traffic lights, lane markings and road signs with impressive accuracy. What causes trouble is not the ordinary journey but the unusual one: the child who runs into the street chasing a ball, the roadworks that force traffic into a temporary lane, the police officer waving drivers through a red light after an accident. Human drivers are not perfect in such situations, of course, but they are often good at guessing what others are about to do. Teaching that kind of judgement to software has proved far harder than early developers expected.

Because of this, some companies have moved away from grand promises. Instead of trying to build vehicles that can handle every road in every weather condition, they are concentrating on limited environments where fewer surprises appear. This is why airports, business parks and ports have become important testing grounds. A shuttle that drives slowly around a predictable route may not sound revolutionary, but supporters argue that such services matter. They can cut costs, reduce emissions and provide useful evidence about how people respond when no one is sitting behind the wheel.

Public reaction, in fact, may shape the future of the technology as much as computer design does. Surveys often show a contradiction: people like the idea of fewer crashes caused by tired or distracted drivers, but they also say they would feel nervous entering a vehicle controlled entirely by software. That hesitation is not simply fear of new technology. It also reflects a difficult question of responsibility. If a driverless bus makes a serious mistake, who should be blamed: the passenger, the operator, the manufacturer or the programmer who wrote the code? Until governments answer that clearly, widespread public trust will remain hard to win.

There is also the risk of expecting too much too soon. Some transport experts worry that the phrase driverless transport creates the false impression that humans will soon disappear from the system altogether. In reality, remote supervisors may still need to monitor vehicles, maintenance teams will have to check sensors constantly, and control-room staff may be required to step in when conditions become too complex. In that sense, automation may change human work rather than remove it. The jobs may look different, but people are unlikely to become irrelevant.

Even so, the long-term effects could be significant. For older people or disabled passengers, autonomous services might offer greater independence, especially in areas with poor public transport. Freight companies are also interested in self-driving lorries that could travel long motorway stretches more efficiently. Still, the most sensible view is probably neither excitement nor disappointment. Driverless transport is unlikely to arrive all at once in a dramatic leap. More probably, it will appear bit by bit, in places where it solves specific problems better than existing systems do. The future may indeed be driverless, but only in parts, and only after a great deal of patient human effort.

Text

Small Changes, Less Pressure

Stress is often described as a modern problem, yet it is really a normal human response to pressure. A busy week, an argument with a friend or an approaching deadline can all make the body react as if danger is near. Heart rate increases, attention narrows and muscles tighten. In the short term, that response can be useful, because it helps us focus and take action. Problems begin when the feeling remains long after the difficult moment has passed.

One reason stress feels hard to manage is that it rarely arrives in one dramatic event. More often, it builds through a series of small demands: messages that need answering, chores left unfinished and plans that keep changing. (37) As a result, people may tell themselves they are coping perfectly well, even while they become less patient, sleep less deeply and find simple decisions unusually tiring.

A common reaction is to search for a quick solution. People often imagine that stress will disappear after a holiday, a free weekend or a more organised week. These things can certainly help, but they do not always change daily habits. (38) If someone checks work messages late at night, skips meals and agrees to every request, pressure soon returns, no matter how restful the weekend was.

For that reason, many experts suggest paying attention to routine before looking for dramatic answers. The most effective habits are not always impressive. Getting up at a similar time each day, preparing lunch in advance or taking ten quiet minutes without a screen can all make a difference. (39) When the mind knows what to expect, it spends less energy dealing with unnecessary uncertainty.

Movement matters too, although it does not have to mean hard training at the gym. A brisk walk, a cycle to the shops or even stretching between tasks can interrupt the physical side of stress. (40) That shift is important, because many people remain seated and tense for hours without noticing how uncomfortable they have become.

Another useful skill is learning when to protect your attention. Many of us treat every notification as urgent, even when it is not. (41) Turning off unnecessary alerts for an hour may feel risky at first, but it often reveals that very few things truly require an immediate response. This can be especially helpful for students and workers who need long periods of concentration.

None of this means stress can be removed completely, and that should not be the goal. Some pressure is part of caring about work, relationships and future plans. (42) By noticing patterns and making small adjustments, people can stop stress from controlling the whole day. Managing it is less about becoming calm all the time and more about recovering balance more quickly when life becomes demanding.

Texts

A – Lena Morris

Initially, I joined a local group that monitored otters simply because I enjoyed being outdoors. I had no plan to get involved in conservation in a serious way. However, after one winter survey, I realised how little people noticed what was happening along the riverbanks near our town. Since then, I’ve spent most weekends giving short talks to walkers and anglers, explaining why small disturbances can matter. What surprises me is that success often depends less on dramatic rescue stories and more on dull tasks like recording water quality and persuading landowners to move fencing. It can be frustratingly slow, yet I prefer that to quick campaigns that attract attention for a month and then disappear. If anything, I’ve become more patient, although I still find it hard when funding is cut just as a project begins to show results.

B – Tariq Shah

For years I photographed rare birds for travel magazines, and at first I saw endangered species mainly as subjects for striking images. Meanwhile, guides in several countries kept telling me the same thing: tourists wanted close views, but the pressure was changing the animals’ behaviour. That made me rethink my own role. Now I still take pictures, yet I work under much stricter rules and often put the camera down entirely. Oddly enough, the most useful thing I do is train younger photographers to recognise when they are too near a nest or feeding site. Some complain that the limits spoil the experience, but I remember making similar excuses myself. I’m not against tourism; in fact, local communities may depend on it. Even so, if visitors leave with only one lesson, I hope it is that admiration should never become interference.

C – Megan Doyle

When our fishing business was asked to support a marine protection zone, my first reaction was suspicion. Like many others on the harbour, I assumed the scientists had arrived with tidy theories and no understanding of how families here earn a living. Yet one of them kept coming back, listening rather than lecturing, and gradually a few of us agreed to test seasonal no-fishing areas. The early months were difficult, especially when catches fell and neighbours said we had been naive. Since then, however, the evidence has become harder to ignore: some species are returning, and younger fish are appearing in places that had seemed empty. I still dislike being told what to do by people from outside the community, but I’ve accepted that protecting wildlife and protecting jobs are not always opposite aims. The challenge is convincing others before they see the proof for themselves.

D – Sofia Petrescu

I run a small rescue centre for injured hedgehogs, so people assume I spend my time doing practical work with animals. In reality, a large part of the job is answering messages from well-meaning members of the public who have picked up healthy young ones by mistake. Although that can be exhausting, I try not to sound annoyed, because their concern comes from a good place. What troubles me more is the expectation that a centre like ours can solve the wider problem alone. We patch up the damage, certainly, but road design, garden fencing and pesticide use matter far more in the long term. Recently, I’ve started visiting schools, as children often persuade adults to change habits more effectively than leaflets do. It’s not glamorous, and donations usually increase only after a dramatic story appears online, yet prevention remains the part of the work I value most.
