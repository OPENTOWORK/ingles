Part 5 — Multiple choice reading

Theme: Travel beyond the tourist trail

VISTA ALUMNO

Text

Beyond the Crowds

There was a time when travelling somewhere unfamiliar meant arriving with only a paper map and a few suggestions copied from a guidebook. Now, before most people have even booked a train, they have already watched videos of the best viewpoints, read reviews of the most photogenic cafés and saved a list of "hidden gems" that are, in reality, known to millions. As a result, many travellers have started looking for places that feel less performed and more real. The idea of going "off the tourist trail" has become a goal in itself.

That phrase sounds simple, but it can be misleading. A place does not become more meaningful just because it is harder to find, and avoiding famous sights entirely can be as limiting as following the crowd everywhere. Some visitors become so determined to discover what others have missed that they treat local streets like a competition, measuring success by how few foreign voices they hear. The more thoughtful travellers are usually those who are less interested in claiming discovery and more interested in paying attention. They understand that being somewhere quietly is often more rewarding than announcing that they have found the one part of a city nobody else knows about.

This shift in attitude has practical reasons as well. In many popular destinations, residents are tired of neighbourhoods being turned into backgrounds for other people's photos. Small bakeries that once served local customers can suddenly find queues forming outside because an image went viral. Rent rises, everyday routines become harder and the character visitors came to admire begins to disappear. Choosing lesser-known areas can reduce pressure on the busiest streets, but only if travellers behave differently there too. If people arrive expecting an untouched version of the centre, complete with cheap coffee and picturesque corners, they may simply repeat the same damage in a new location.

So what does exploring beyond the obvious actually involve? Often, it means adjusting expectations rather than travelling further. In Lisbon, for example, many visitors rush between the historic centre and the famous tram routes, while missing ordinary districts where life happens at a slower pace. These areas may not offer perfect views at every turn, but they can reveal how a city fits together: where children play after school, which parks fill up at lunch, how old workshops survive next to new businesses. None of this is dramatic, yet for some travellers that is precisely the attraction. They return home with a stronger sense of place, even if their photos attract less attention online.

Of course, not everyone wants this kind of experience every day. There is nothing wrong with visiting major landmarks, and famous places are often famous for good reasons. The problem begins when travel turns into a checklist or when people confuse novelty with understanding. A spectacular square seen at sunrise can teach you something about a city; so can a quiet market where you understand almost nothing that is being said. What matters is the quality of attention you bring. If you move through a place only searching for confirmation that you are adventurous, you will probably miss what is in front of you.

Perhaps that is why the most satisfying discoveries are often modest. They are not always remote beaches or secret mountain paths, but moments when a place stops feeling like a product and starts feeling inhabited. You notice a mechanic greeting three generations of the same family, or a football match beginning on a patch of rough ground at the edge of town. These scenes are easy to overlook because they do not fit a neat travel story. Yet they may stay in the memory longer than the celebrated attraction nearby. Going beyond the tourist trail, then, is less about geography than about learning to look with patience, respect and curiosity.

Part 6 — Gapped text

Theme: Teenagers & social media

VISTA ALUMNO

Text

Growing Up Online

For today’s teenagers, social media is not a separate activity that begins when homework is finished. It is woven into everyday life, shaping how friendships are maintained, how news is discovered and even how people see themselves. Adults often focus on the dangers, while young users point to the fun and freedom these platforms provide. In reality, both views contain some truth. To understand the effect of social media on teenagers, it is necessary to look beyond headlines and examine how it influences daily habits.

One reason these apps matter so much is that they offer a constant connection to other people. A teenager can share a joke, post a photo or ask for advice within seconds, and the response is often immediate. (37) That ability to share everyday worries can be especially valuable after moving to a new area or changing schools. Keeping in touch online may help them feel less isolated and more confident about starting conversations in person.

However, the same tools that create connection can also increase pressure. Many teenagers are highly aware of how their posts will be judged, not only by close friends but by classmates and even strangers. (38) As a result, a simple photo can be edited repeatedly before it is shared, and a message may be rewritten several times to avoid sounding awkward. What appears to be relaxed communication can involve a surprising amount of performance.

Another issue is the speed at which ideas spread online. Trends, opinions and rumours can travel through a school year group in minutes, leaving little time to check whether they are true. (39) A minor disagreement that might once have ended after school can continue late into the evening through group chats and comment sections. When there is no clear break from the conversation, small problems can quickly feel much bigger.

Sleep is affected too, though not always for the reasons adults imagine. It is not simply that teenagers choose entertainment over rest. Notifications, fear of missing out and the habit of checking one more post can make it hard to stop. (40) Over time, this broken pattern may leave students tired in lessons and less able to concentrate, even if they believe they slept for a reasonable number of hours.

That said, social media is not only a source of distraction or anxiety. Many teenagers use it to explore interests that are not shared by people around them. They can find artists, sports fans or young activists from other parts of the world and learn from them. (41) In some cases, online spaces also allow quieter students to express ideas more comfortably than they would in a busy classroom.

So what should parents and schools do? Banning social media completely is unlikely to succeed, partly because online interaction has become a normal part of modern social life. (42) Teenagers need practical strategies, such as turning off alerts at night, checking sources before sharing stories and recognising when an app is affecting their mood. The goal is not to persuade young people to reject social media, but to help them use it with more awareness and control.

A – Leah Morgan

I took up pottery by accident after a friend dragged me to an evening class. At the time, my job in events meant I was constantly dealing with last-minute problems, so I liked the idea of making something slowly with my hands. Initially, I was terrible at it and felt ridiculous sitting beside people who seemed to know exactly what they were doing. However, the teacher never corrected us too quickly, and that made me less afraid of getting things wrong. Since then, pottery has altered the way I deal with the rest of my life. I still rush around at work, yet I no longer expect everything to be perfect straight away. Oddly enough, the best part isn't taking finished bowls home, but the quiet concentration in the studio, where nobody asks anything of me for two hours.

B – Owen Patel

For years, I laughed at people who spent weekends watching birds through binoculars. Then my daughter asked for help with a school project on garden wildlife, and I went along with it just to support her. Meanwhile, I found myself getting up early to see what landed in the park near our flat. What began as a favour turned into a habit that changed my routine completely. Before, I used to spend Sundays checking emails and worrying about Monday; now I leave my phone behind and head outdoors. I wouldn't claim birdwatching has made me calmer in every situation, although my family says it has. What it has certainly done is sharpen my attention. I notice weather, sound and movement far more than I ever used to, and that sense of being properly present has stayed with me.

C – Rosa Bell

When I started running, it was mainly because my doctor told me I needed regular exercise. I wasn't inspired by stories of marathon runners, and in truth I expected to give up within a month. Yet joining a local group changed everything. There were people of every age and ability, and no one made me feel slow. Gradually, training runs became the point around which I organised my week. The biggest surprise, though, was not the physical change but the social one. After moving to a new town, I had found it hard to meet people beyond work, whereas through running I built real friendships. We still talk about races, of course, but we also help one another through ordinary life. These days I still enjoy improving my times, although I know companionship is the reason I keep going back.

D – Marcus Reid

I bought a second-hand guitar during a difficult winter when I'd been told to stay home and recover from an injury. At first, learning online felt frustrating because I couldn't ask anyone what I was doing wrong, and progress seemed painfully slow. Even so, practising gave shape to days that had previously felt empty. Later, when I was able to leave the house again, I nearly stopped. Then a neighbour invited me to a small music night at a café, and hearing amateurs play without embarrassment convinced me to carry on. I still wouldn't perform alone, yet I've joined them a few times and discovered I enjoy contributing to something shared. In that sense, guitar changed my life twice: first by helping me through isolation, and later by giving me the confidence to take part instead of standing at the edge.
