Part 5 — Multiple-choice reading

Theme: Recycling systems and waste

VISTA ALUMNO

Read the text and choose the answer (A, B, C or D) which you think fits best according to the text.

Text

Does Recycling Really Work?

For years, recycling has been presented as one of the simplest ways for ordinary people to help the planet. Rinse a jar, flatten a box, put a bottle in the right bin, and feel that a small but useful action has been taken. Yet the subject has become far more complicated than those early public campaigns suggested. Reports about plastic waste being exported abroad, or about carefully separated rubbish ending up in the same lorry, have led many people to ask an uncomfortable question: if the system is so messy, is recycling really effective at all?

The short answer is yes, but only when it is part of a much bigger picture. Recycling does save raw materials and energy in many cases. Producing aluminium from recycled metal, for example, uses far less energy than making it from the original ore. Glass and paper can also bring clear benefits when they are collected and processed properly. The difficulty is that people often imagine recycling as a magical solution that cancels out wasteful habits. In reality, it is more like damage control: useful, necessary, but limited.

Part of the confusion comes from the fact that not all materials behave in the same way. A metal can often be recycled repeatedly with little loss in quality, while many plastics become weaker each time they are reprocessed. Some plastic items cannot be economically recycled at all, especially if they contain different layers or food remains. This does not mean that all recycling programmes are dishonest. It means that the famous chasing-arrows symbol can suggest a promise that the real system cannot always keep. Something may be technically recyclable but still unlikely to be recycled in practice.

Another reason for public doubt is that recycling depends on markets, not just good intentions. A council may collect a material separately, but that material still needs a buyer. If the price of new plastic falls, factories may prefer it to recycled plastic, which can be less consistent in quality. When that happens, a perfectly organised local collection system may suddenly become less effective. Critics sometimes use these failures to argue that recycling is pointless. That is too simple. The better conclusion is that recycling needs stable investment, clear rules for manufacturers and products designed with reuse in mind.

There is also a psychological problem. Once people believe they are recycling, they may feel they have done their part and stop asking harder questions. A coffee cup dropped into the correct bin can seem like a success, even if that cup contains materials that are difficult to separate. In that sense, recycling can become a comforting story that protects overconsumption. Businesses know this. Some companies have promoted recyclable packaging while continuing to produce unnecessary single-use products. They are not exactly lying, but they are directing attention towards the final stage of a product's life and away from the amount being produced in the first place.

None of this means household recycling is a waste of time. On the contrary, well-run schemes remain an important tool, especially for materials such as metals, paper and glass. But expecting bins alone to solve the waste crisis is like expecting seat belts to solve dangerous driving. They reduce harm, yet they do not remove the cause of the problem. If governments want real progress, they must encourage repair, refill systems and longer-lasting products, while making manufacturers responsible for what happens after sale. Recycling works best not as the hero of the story, but as one member of a much larger team.

Theme: Return of home cooking

Six sentences have been removed from the article. Choose from the sentences A–G the one which fits each gap. There is one extra sentence which you do not need to use.

Text

Why Home Cooking Is Back

For years, the story of modern eating seemed simple: people were busier than ever, takeaway apps were growing fast, and cooking at home looked old-fashioned. Yet recently, the opposite trend has begun to attract attention. More people are planning meals, buying basic ingredients and sharing photos of dishes they have made themselves. It is not just a hobby for skilled food lovers, either. Ordinary households are starting to see cooking as a normal part of daily life again.

One reason is clearly financial. Meals bought outside the home have become noticeably more expensive, especially in towns and cities where food delivery is common. Families who once relied on prepared food a few nights a week are now doing the maths more carefully. (37) As a result, dishes that were once seen as slow or inconvenient, such as soups, pasta sauces and rice-based meals, suddenly make practical sense again.

Money, however, is only part of the explanation. During periods when people spent more time at home, many discovered that cooking could structure the day and provide a small sense of progress. Chopping vegetables or preparing dough offered a break from screens and constant messages. (38) For some, that confidence remained even after life became busier again, and they continued to cook because they now felt capable rather than nervous.

The internet has also changed the experience in ways that make home cooking easier to begin. In the past, a beginner might have been put off by a recipe full of unfamiliar terms or assumed knowledge. Now, short videos can show every step, from holding a knife correctly to checking whether onions are cooked enough. (39) That kind of guidance reduces the risk of wasting ingredients, which matters even more when budgets are tight.

Another important change involves ideas about health. People have become more aware of how often they eat highly processed food and how little control they have over what goes into it. At home, even simple cooking gives them more influence over salt, sugar and portion size. (40) Instead, they are aiming for meals that feel realistic on a weekday but still healthier than something bought in a hurry.

There is also a social side to the return of home cooking. In some households, preparing dinner has become a shared task rather than one person’s responsibility. Children wash vegetables, flatmates take turns choosing recipes, and friends invite one another over for low-cost meals. (41) It turns cooking into an activity with conversation and routine, rather than a lonely job that has to be finished as quickly as possible.

Of course, not everyone has the time, space or energy to cook regularly, and ready-made food remains useful for many people. Even so, the renewed interest in home cooking seems likely to last. (42) In that sense, the return of home cooking is not really about going backwards. It is about finding a habit that suits modern life better than many people expected.

Theme: Career choices and changing direction

VISTA ALUMNO

Read the article in which people talk about their experiences. For each question, choose from the people (A–D). The people may be chosen more than once.

Which person talks about each of the following? The people may be chosen more than once.

43 Who realised from an activity outside their main job what kind of work suited them better?

44 Who says that outsiders fail to appreciate how important their work can be?

45 Who advises people not to be too influenced by other people's expectations?

46 Who mentions that their current role still makes use of knowledge gained earlier?

47 Who chose a path mainly because their personal circumstances limited the alternatives?

48 Who accepted a less stable income in exchange for feeling happier about work?

49 Who discovered that the part of work they enjoyed most was not the one they were originally employed to do?

50 Who stayed in a suitable profession for some time despite a sense that it was not really right for them?

51 Who talks about enjoying work because it combines routine with the unexpected?

52 Who is considering becoming independent in their field at a later stage?

Texts

A – Leah Morton

At school I was the sort of person who wanted a plan for everything, so choosing a career felt strangely difficult. I did well in science and everyone assumed I would study medicine, yet I wasn't convinced it suited me. During a summer job at a local garden centre, I realised I enjoyed explaining things to customers far more than dealing with stock. That surprised me. Initially, I ignored it and started a pharmacy course because it seemed sensible. However, after six months I switched to training as a science teacher. Since then, I've never regretted it. The pay isn't amazing and the workload can be intense, especially in winter, but I like the mix of structure and unpredictability. Young people ask honest questions, and that keeps me interested. Looking back, I wish I'd paid less attention to what other people thought was impressive.

B – Ravi Sen

My route into work was less about passion and more about necessity. My family ran a small café, and as the oldest child I helped out from the age of thirteen. By the time I left school, I knew I could manage people and solve problems quickly, but university wasn't financially realistic. Meanwhile, one of our suppliers suggested I apply for an apprenticeship in logistics. I had no idea what the sector involved, yet I liked the fact that every day brought a different challenge. Ten years on, I coordinate deliveries for a food company. Some friends still think it sounds dull compared with creative jobs, although I disagree. If a shipment goes wrong, shops can end up with empty shelves, so the pressure is real. I may eventually set up my own business, but for now I'm learning as much as I can from experienced colleagues.

C – Nina Kowalska

I spent years trying to choose between secure work and something more personal. As a teenager I loved drawing clothes, but people kept warning me that fashion was too competitive. In the end, I trained as an accountant and stayed in the profession longer than expected because I was good at it. Even so, I always felt slightly detached from what I was doing. Then, while making costumes for a local theatre group in the evenings, I noticed how absorbed I became when time was short and a design problem needed fixing. That was a clue I couldn't ignore. I now run a small alterations studio and design custom pieces at weekends. The income is less predictable than before, which can be worrying. Yet I prefer that uncertainty to spending every Monday counting the days until Friday. For me, choosing the right path meant accepting that comfort isn't the same as satisfaction.

D – Owen Price

People often imagine that choosing a career is a single dramatic moment, but mine changed through a series of small decisions. I studied computer engineering and joined a large tech company straight after university. The office was modern, the salary was decent and the promotion route was clear. Still, after a while I found myself enjoying the training sessions more than the technical work. I liked helping new staff understand difficult systems and watching them become more confident. At first I treated that as a useful side task; later I realised it was the part of the week I looked forward to most. Two years ago I moved into corporate learning and now design training programmes for different departments. Oddly enough, I use my original degree every day, just not in the way I expected. That has taught me that changing direction doesn't always mean starting from zero.
