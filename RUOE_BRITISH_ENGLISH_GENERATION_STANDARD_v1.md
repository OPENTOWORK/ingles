# RUOE British English generation standard

**Version:** v1 — draft only. Not wired into runtime.  
**Status:** proposed shared contract for a future English-first prompt layer.

This document is the canonical language standard. It is written in British English because the model must think, judge, and write in British English. Do not translate it into Spanish for use as a generation or validation prompt.

---

## 1. Standing rule

Think, evaluate, and write in British English throughout.

Do not plan the item in another language and then translate the result.

**Natural British English before exercise convenience.**

Before accepting any generated sentence, ask:

**Would a competent British English speaker naturally produce this sentence?**

If the answer is no, change the item. Do not twist the English so that a planned gap, keyword, stem, or transformation still fits.

**Naturalness takes priority over preserving a planned target. If a target cannot be tested naturally, change the item rather than forcing the English.**

---

## 2. British spelling

Use British spelling in every student-facing sentence and in every option.

- organised, recognised, travelled, cancelled
- colour, favour, honour, behaviour
- programme (a plan or a broadcast), centre, metre, theatre
- practise (verb), practice (noun); licence (noun), license (verb)
- analogue, catalogue where those forms are still normal

Do not mix British and American spellings inside one text.

## 3. British vocabulary

Prefer the word a British speaker would choose in that situation.

| Use | Do not force |
|---|---|
| flat, lift, pavement, holiday, autumn, queue, timetable, rubbish, trainer (shoe), motorway | apartment, elevator, sidewalk, vacation, fall, line (for a queue), schedule (when *timetable* is the natural word), garbage, sneaker |
| at the weekend, in hospital, have a shower | on the weekend, in the hospital (when British usage omits *the*), take a shower as the only form |

American vocabulary is a defect when a normal British alternative exists. It is not a defect when the text is deliberately about American life and the voice is marked as such. RUOE articles are not that case: write them as British English.

## 4. Collocation

Test the partnership, not a gloss.

A collocation is acceptable only if people say it in that exact frame.

- Natural: *heavy rain*, *make a decision*, *take part*, *meet a deadline*, *highly unlikely*, *bitterly disappointed*.
- Unnatural: *strong rain* as a “near miss” that is still offered as a serious option; *do a photo*; *open the light*; *make a photo*.

If the only way to create a wrong option is to use a partnership nobody would say, the item is badly designed. Change the sentence.

## 5. Lexical precision

Near-synonyms must be distinguished by the sentence, not by a dictionary note.

*Delay*, *postpone*, *cancel*, and *put off* are not interchangeable. *Postpone* a meeting. *Delay* a train by forty minutes. If two of those words both produce a sentence a British speaker would accept, the item is ambiguous.

## 6. No translationese

Reject sentences that sound translated, even when they are grammatical.

- Not: *I am agree.* *He made me a surprise.* *We discussed about the plan.* *It has a lot of importance.*
- Write: *I agree.* *He gave me a surprise* / *He surprised me.* *We discussed the plan.* *It matters a great deal* / *It is very important.*

Do not open with *Nowadays, X is very important* or *X has become increasingly popular in recent years* unless the sentence is doing real work, which it almost never is.

Avoid corporate filler: *leverage*, *holistic approach*, *stakeholders* used as decoration, *it is important to note*.

## 7. Sentence naturalness

Every completed sentence must sound like something from a magazine, a newspaper, a website, or ordinary speech at B2 — not like a grammar drill.

- The gap, the keyword, or the stem comes second. The sentence comes first.
- Do not add a clause only to host a gap.
- Do not leave a verb hanging (*She decided.*) when the idea is incomplete.
- Read the finished sentence aloud. If you would not say it, or would only say it to save the exercise, rewrite it.

## 8. Cambridge-style register

Match B2 First, not C1 academic prose and not A2 textbook English.

- Parts 1–3 and 5–6: a clear general-reader article.
- Part 4: two plausible sentences about a real situation, not slogans.
- Part 7: four distinguishable people. No shared “Emma changes career” template unless the brief demands it.
- Directions stay in the existing Cambridge wording. Do not paraphrase the official instruction into something chattier.

## 9. Distractor uniqueness (Part 1)

This rule overrides any older line that says a distractor may “make sense as an idea” and fail only because it is “less natural”.

**Substitute A, B, C and D independently into the complete sentence.**

Reject the item if more than one option produces:

- natural British English;
- grammatical English;
- semantically defensible English;
- an acceptable collocation or fixed expression in that exact context.

**A distractor is not acceptable merely because it is less idiomatic than the key. If it is still genuinely natural and semantically defensible in the sentence, reject the item.**

Do not repair that item by declaring the key “more idiomatic”. Repair the sentence or the option set, or regenerate the item.

Absurd options are also unacceptable (*the rain decided heavily*). They are a different failure. Removing them is not enough.

### Part 1 — good item

Sentence with the gap:

*The 8.15 to Leeds was _____ by nearly forty minutes.*

- A) delayed
- B) postponed
- C) cancelled
- D) avoided

Completed sentences:

- *The 8.15 to Leeds was delayed by nearly forty minutes.* Natural. This is the key.
- *The 8.15 to Leeds was postponed by nearly forty minutes.* Not what a British speaker says about a train that is running late. *Postpone* does not take this time phrase in this way.
- *The 8.15 to Leeds was cancelled by nearly forty minutes.* Ungrammatical as a measure of lateness.
- *The 8.15 to Leeds was avoided by nearly forty minutes.* Not a meaningful sentence.

One option survives. The item may stand.

### Part 1 — bad item (reject)

*We still need to _____ a decision before Friday.*

- A) make
- B) take
- C) do
- D) have

*Make a decision* and *take a decision* are both natural British English. *Do* and *have* are not. The item is still ambiguous, because two options work. Do not keep it on the grounds that *make* is more frequent in coursebooks.

## 10. Semantic equivalence (Part 4)

Sentence 1 and the completed sentence 2 must mean the same thing.

Reject a grammatically possible answer that changes:

- certainty (*must* / *might*);
- time reference;
- scope (*all* / *most*);
- who does the action;
- degree (*quite* / *very*);
- politeness, where that changes the force of the sentence.

There is one defensible route. Do not list a second grammatical transformation as another full answer.

## 11. Ambiguity policy (all parts)

One defensible answer per scored item.

- Part 1: the substitution test in §9.
- Part 2: if a second single word is fully correct (*who* / *that* in a defining relative clause; *though* / *however* in the same slot), the gap is illegal. Gap something else.
- Part 3: one derived word that is natural in the sentence. A form that only adds *-s* or *-ed* to the stem is not word formation.
- Part 5: one option fully supported by the text. A tempting misread is allowed. A second reading that the text equally supports is not.
- Part 6: one sentence completes the gap. If another pool sentence also completes it coherently, rewrite.
- Part 7: one person is clearly the best match. Paraphrase the stem. Do not copy four or more consecutive words from a profile.

## 12. Word-formation naturalness (Part 3)

The derived word must be the word a writer would have chosen in that sentence, not a suffix parked in a gap.

Accept *long → length*, *decide → decisive*, *anxious → anxiety* when the sentence truly needs that word.

Reject *perform → perform*. Reject a sentence that exists only so that an unusual prefix will fit.

## 13. Part 4 equivalence and marking

The two sentences are ordinary British English. The marking points describe that answer; they do not excuse an unnatural sentence.

Contractions that British speakers use (*do not* / *don't*, *cannot* / *can't*) may be controlled variants. They are not a second transformation.

If the answer cannot be split into two honest marking points without leftover words, change the item.

## 14. Discourse cohesion (Parts 5–7)

- Part 5 distractors come from ideas that are actually in the passage. They fail because of scope, relation, attitude, or a detail — not because they are nonsense.
- Part 6 sentences sound like sentences removed from the article. Links (*this*, *however*, *by then*) must work backwards and forwards. Keyword echo alone is not cohesion.
- Part 7 profiles overlap in theme and differ in judgement. Questions paraphrase. They do not reuse the profile’s wording as a clue.

A Part 5 distractor that tempts a candidate is doing its job. A Part 1 distractor that remains natural British English is not. Do not copy the Part 5 “tempting is fine” rule onto Part 1.

## 15. Paraphrase distance (Part 7)

The stem must not lift a four-word chain from any profile.

Prefer a line someone would write on an exam paper:

- Weak rewrite: *Who struggled to cope when a work deadline approached without guidance?*
- Better: *Who found it hard to cope when a deadline loomed and nobody offered any help?*

The better line is still B2, and it does not sound translated.

## 16. Good and bad items (short set)

**Good — Part 4 meaning holds**

- Sentence 1: *You don’t have to wear a tie at this office.*
- Keyword: NEED
- Sentence 2: *You _____ a tie at this office.*
- Answer: *don’t need to wear* / *do not need to wear*

Both sentences are natural. The meaning matches. The keyword is unchanged.

**Bad — Part 4 meaning slips**

- Sentence 1: *I’m sure she took the earlier train.*
- A completion that means only *She might have taken the earlier train* is grammatical and still wrong. Certainty has changed. Reject it.

**Good — Part 2 one word**

*The museum is closed _____ Mondays.* → *on*

*In*, *at*, and *during* do not fit this British English frame equally. One answer.

**Bad — Part 2 two relatives**

*The people _____ work here are very helpful.* → both *who* and *that* are correct. Do not set this gap.

**Bad — translationese forced into a gap**

*She _____ a photo of the castle with her phone.* with key *made*.

British English says *took a photo*. Forcing *made* to host a collocation item produces the wrong English. Change the item.

---

## 17. What this standard does not change

It does not change question counts, numbering, the 2–5 word rule, keyword integrity, gap markers, Style Card IDs, Transformation Family IDs, Content Briefs, the Topic Bank, Part 6 Architecture v2, or Part 4 metadata schema.

Those stay mechanical. This standard governs the English that fills them.
