RUOE STYLE CARDS

B2 Reading and Use of English · Content Engine

Version 1.0 · 10 August 2026

Purpose

Define reusable editorial families for generation of Content Briefs and RUOE texts.

Source corpus

RUOE CambridgeOne sample texts supplied for style analysis.

Current status

Six cards written and referenced; ready for practical pilot validation.



1. Operating principles

These cards describe recurring editorial mechanisms observed across authentic B2 RUOE samples. They are not templates to copy and they do not replace the structural prompt of any Part.

• The Topic Bank decides the thematic raw material; the Style Card decides how that material can be shaped editorially; the Content Brief makes the specific editorial decisions for one exercise.

• A Style Card must remain reusable across topics. It should define opening logic, progression, substance, voice, closing function and typical risks—not fixed facts or fixed storylines.

• Parts 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 can share a style family, but the structural prompt still controls length, item type, numbering and exam mechanics.

• Part 7 uses the dedicated Multiple Profiles & Testimonies family because its content must be distributed across four comparable but distinct voices/profiles.

• Part 4 does not use Style Cards.

• References are evidence for mechanisms only. Wording, named people, statistics and narrative details must never be copied into generated content.

Card fields

Each card contains the approved minimum fields: Name; What content it suits; Compatible Parts; Typical opening; Content progression; What gives it real substance; Tone/voice; Typical ending; Avoid; References used.

Status logic

The six cards below are complete enough to enter the pilot. They should be treated as Ready for pilot validation—not yet as empirically validated—until they have generated new Content Briefs/texts and passed human review.



SC-01  ·  STYLE CARD

Curiosity & Explanatory Article

STATUSReady for pilot validation

LANGUAGEBritish English

LEVELCEFR B2

What content it suits

Questions, phenomena, behaviours, natural/scientific curiosities and everyday mechanisms that can be explained clearly without becoming academic.

Compatible Parts

Parts 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6.

Typical opening

• Begin with a familiar observation, surprising fact, accessible question or apparent contradiction.

• Create an immediate knowledge gap: the reader should understand what is interesting before receiving the explanation.

• Avoid opening with a dry definition unless the definition itself is surprising or necessary.

Content progression

• Move from the familiar phenomenon to the underlying explanation.

• Introduce one or more concrete examples, mechanisms, observations or pieces of evidence.

• Where useful, present an initial assumption and then refine, complicate or correct it.

• Build paragraphs so each one answers a new part of the reader’s implicit question rather than restating the same idea.

• For short Parts 1–3, compress this into a miniature arc: hook → explanation → one concrete development → meaningful closing point.

What gives it real substance

• A precise phenomenon rather than a broad theme.

• Specific mechanisms, causes, consequences or contrasts.

• Concrete examples that make the explanation memorable.

• Careful factuality: plausible general explanations are acceptable, but invented studies, institutions, statistics or quotations are not.

Tone / voice

Accessible, intelligent and curious. Neutral-to-engaged rather than textbook-like. The writer sounds interested in explaining something to a general reader, not lecturing them.

Typical ending

• Close by resolving the initial curiosity, revealing a wider implication, or showing why the phenomenon matters.

• An effective final sentence may slightly broaden the perspective, but should not become generic advice or a moral.

Avoid

• Encyclopaedia-style lists of facts with no progression.

• Overloading the text with technical terminology.

• Generic openings such as 'Nowadays, X is very important'.

• Unsupported claims presented as research findings.

• Repeating the same explanatory point in different wording.

• Turning an explanation into self-help.

References used

5.2 — Birth order – does it make any difference?. Strong model of question-led explanation, competing ideas, evidence and a conclusion that remains intellectually open rather than simplistic.

2.2 — Amazing fungi!. Compact curiosity-led explanation: familiar object → hidden reality → striking example → current area of interest.

3.2 — The grass-eating shark. Uses a surprising natural phenomenon, competing explanations and concrete biological detail to sustain curiosity.

ENGINE NOTE  Use the card to shape the editorial arc only. Any statistics, studies or factual claims in a generated brief must be verifiable or deliberately framed as non-specific/plausible rather than invented.



SC-02  ·  STYLE CARD

Trend & Social Report

STATUSReady for pilot validation

LANGUAGEBritish English

LEVELCEFR B2

What content it suits

Changes in habits, technology, work, education, environment, leisure or social behaviour where the interest comes from what is changing, why it is changing and what effects or tensions follow.

Compatible Parts

Parts 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6.

Typical opening

• Start from a recognisable change, emerging practice, new concern or contrast between past and present.

• Show the social relevance immediately: what people are doing differently, what is becoming common, or what problem is attracting attention.

• The opening can use a concrete scene or example before widening to the trend.

Content progression

• Establish the trend or change clearly.

• Explain drivers, motivations or conditions behind it.

• Develop consequences, benefits, drawbacks or different reactions.

• Add one or two concrete cases, settings or stakeholder perspectives to prevent abstraction.

• End with an implication, unresolved tension or realistic direction of travel rather than a sweeping prediction.

What gives it real substance

• Observable change over time or contrast between groups/contexts.

• Specific behaviours and consequences, not vague claims about 'society'.

• Balanced tensions: convenience vs cost, innovation vs unintended effect, popularity vs drawback, etc.

• Concrete examples that make the trend visible at human scale.

Tone / voice

Magazine/reporting voice: informed, current-feeling and readable. Balanced rather than promotional or alarmist. May include mild interpretive commentary while remaining grounded.

Typical ending

• Close on what the trend means in practice, what remains uncertain, or how people/institutions are adapting.

• Avoid absolute predictions. A small unresolved question or tension often works better.

Avoid

• Invented percentages or surveys merely to make the text sound authoritative.

• Technology-is-good / technology-is-bad simplifications.

• Trend pieces with no people, settings or consequences.

• Overusing 'more and more people' without showing what has actually changed.

• Journalistic sensationalism or moral panic.

References used

6.2 — Mini forests. A social/environmental innovation presented through concept, practical implementation, community relevance and wider benefits.

5.2 — Birth order – does it make any difference?. Useful for showing how established social beliefs can be tested and revised through newer evidence.

1.2 — Breaking bad habits. Compact social/behavioural explanation built around a common assumption, expert perspective and practical implication.

ENGINE NOTE  The card does not require 'news'. A trend can be long-running or emerging; what matters is a clear pattern of change plus consequences. The Topic Bank supplies the domain; the Content Brief decides the specific angle.



SC-03  ·  STYLE CARD

Personal Reflective Experience

STATUSReady for pilot validation

LANGUAGEBritish English

LEVELCEFR B2

What content it suits

First-person experiences in which the value comes from a change in understanding, a decision, an unexpected difficulty, a discovered preference or a personal reinterpretation of something familiar.

Compatible Parts

Parts 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6.

Typical opening

• Open from a personal situation, habit, expectation or decision that gives the reader a concrete starting point.

• Signal the narrator’s attitude early, but leave room for that attitude to change.

• Small personal details are preferable to a generic autobiographical introduction.

Content progression

• Set up the narrator’s initial expectation or normal behaviour.

• Introduce an experience, obstacle, discovery or contradiction.

• Show the narrator reacting, adapting or reassessing.

• Use concrete episodes rather than summarising the whole experience abstractly.

• Finish with what changed in the narrator’s view, behaviour or sense of value.

What gives it real substance

• Specific moments, choices, frustrations, surprises and sensory or situational detail.

• A clear before/after contrast in perspective.

• Self-awareness: the narrator can admit uncertainty, embarrassment, mistaken assumptions or mixed feelings.

• Reflection should grow out of events; it should not be pasted on as a life lesson.

Tone / voice

Natural first-person magazine voice. Conversational but controlled; reflective without sounding therapeutic. Light humour, self-deprecation and small asides are welcome when natural.

Typical ending

• Close on a changed perspective, earned satisfaction, lingering question or concrete intention.

• The ending should feel specific to the experience, not like a universal motivational slogan.

Avoid

• Generic 'this taught me to never give up' morals.

• Chronological diary-style listing with no change in perspective.

• Overdramatic emotional language.

• Perfect narrators who never make mistakes or reconsider anything.

• Reflection that is not supported by concrete events.

References used

5.1 — My book collection. Excellent reflective arc: attachment → embarrassment/problem → attempted solution → rediscovery → more nuanced understanding of self and others.

5.5 — My first summer job. Expectation versus reality, several concrete episodes, adaptation and a final moment where the narrator discovers a genuine strength.

6.6 — The wild attraction of competitive fell running. Personal entry point, remembered first encounter, development into commitment and an explicit but experience-based explanation of why the activity matters.

ENGINE NOTE  For Parts 1–3, keep the narrator and change-of-perspective mechanism but reduce the number of episodes. For Part 6, the progression must also provide strong discourse links between paragraphs for the structural prompt to exploit.



SC-04  ·  STYLE CARD

Narrative Story

STATUSReady for pilot validation

LANGUAGEBritish English

LEVELCEFR B2

What content it suits

Character-centred stories, biographical episodes and narrative extracts where events reveal personality, conflict, motivation or change. The emphasis is on scene, action and consequence rather than explanation.

Compatible Parts

Parts 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6.

Typical opening

• Enter through a scene, action, concrete setting or character detail rather than background exposition.

• Establish who matters, where we are and what kind of tension or desire is present.

• Use selective detail that hints at character before explaining it explicitly.

Content progression

• Move through causally connected events rather than a list of facts.

• Reveal character through decisions, reactions, dialogue, objects and contrasts.

• Introduce a complication, obstacle, discovery or turning point.

• Allow earlier details to acquire significance later.

• End with consequence, changed situation, revealing reaction or a meaningful unresolved note.

What gives it real substance

• Specific physical setting and purposeful details.

• Character motivation and viewpoint.

• Cause-and-effect between events.

• Small tensions, contrasts or setbacks that create forward movement.

• For biographical narrative, select only episodes that contribute to the central arc.

Tone / voice

Immersive but B2-accessible. Concrete, economical and observant. Dialogue or interior judgement can be used sparingly. Description supports character/action rather than becoming ornamental.

Typical ending

• Close on a consequence or character-revealing moment rather than summarising the story.

• In shorter narrative-biographical texts, the ending can show the resulting career/state, provided the route to it has been developed.

Avoid

• Backstory dumps before anything happens.

• Too many named characters or subplots.

• Artificial cliffhangers.

• Decorative description with no narrative function.

• Events that happen randomly without affecting the character.

• Highly literary C1/C2 prose or difficult figurative density.

References used

5.3 — Roy Mason / beach-hut novel extract. Strong scene-led narrative voice: setting, objects and reactions reveal Roy’s values without explanatory exposition.

1.4 — Adriano Spano’s story. Compressed biographical narrative built around desire, training, career movement and fulfilment of a long-held ambition.

3.6 — A happy and successful chef. First-person career narrative with early motivation, obstacles, resourcefulness and a concrete outcome.

ENGINE NOTE  This family is not an invitation to create fiction in every Part. It covers both fictional narrative and factual/plausible biographical storytelling. The Content Brief determines the factuality level.



SC-05  ·  STYLE CARD

Culture, Travel & Community Feature

STATUSReady for pilot validation

LANGUAGEBritish English

LEVELCEFR B2

What content it suits

Places, local practices, cultural activities, heritage, travel experiences, communities and outdoor pursuits where the setting is an active part of the content rather than a decorative backdrop.

Compatible Parts

Parts 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6.

Typical opening

• Anchor the reader quickly in a place, local activity, landmark or distinctive community practice.

• Use one concrete feature—landscape, ritual, object, event or local behaviour—to make the setting immediate.

• Where personal voice is used, connect the narrator’s reason for being there to the place itself.

Content progression

• Move from orientation to discovery: where/what → distinctive features → people or activity → meaning/impact.

• Let the physical or cultural setting shape what happens rather than merely hosting unrelated content.

• Combine descriptive detail with explanation, experience or community context.

• Where appropriate, contrast visitor expectation with local reality or past with present.

What gives it real substance

• Place-specific details that could not simply be transplanted to any location.

• Local practices, landscape, heritage, community relationships or practical constraints.

• A human reason to care: identity, belonging, discovery, preservation, challenge, enjoyment or change.

• Concrete details over tourist-brochure adjectives.

Tone / voice

Warm, observant feature-writing. Curious and respectful. Can be first-person or third-person. Descriptive passages remain controlled and functional at B2.

Typical ending

• Close by returning to the value of the place/activity, a changed perception, a community implication or a vivid final experience.

• Avoid generic 'everyone should visit' conclusions.

Avoid

• Tourist-brochure language and strings of superlatives.

• Generic travel descriptions that could describe anywhere.

• Reducing cultures or communities to stereotypes.

• Inventing heritage claims or local facts.

• Description without progression or human relevance.

References used

6.1 — Touring round artworks – by bike!. Integrates travel/activity, landscape and public art so each location changes the experience and the writer’s interpretation.

6.6 — The wild attraction of competitive fell running. The activity is inseparable from northern English landscape, local tradition, weather and community organisation.

2.6 — A famous landmark in Australia / Uluru. Compact place-based text combining location, physical features, indigenous significance and current visitor behaviour.

ENGINE NOTE  A culture/travel/community brief should specify what makes the setting functionally important. If the same text would work unchanged after swapping the place name, the brief is too generic.



SC-06  ·  STYLE CARD

Multiple Profiles & Testimonies

STATUSReady for pilot validation

LANGUAGEBritish English

LEVELCEFR B2

What content it suits

Part 7 content built from four people/profiles who share a common theme but differ in experience, attitude, motivation, difficulty, response or outcome.

Compatible Parts

Part 7.

Typical opening

• Use a concise shared frame or title that makes the comparison domain clear.

• Each profile should enter quickly through a distinctive personal fact, attitude, episode or problem rather than repeating the shared topic.

• Profiles do not need identical openings; controlled variation helps voice differentiation.

Content progression

• Give each profile a self-contained mini-arc with 2–4 comparable dimensions.

• Distribute overlapping concepts across profiles so questions require discrimination rather than keyword spotting.

• Make some profiles share surface similarities while differing in cause, attitude, timing, motivation or consequence.

• Ensure every profile contains multiple examinable ideas and that no single profile becomes obviously richer than the others.

• Across the set, vary the order in which comparable dimensions appear.

What gives it real substance

• Concrete episodes and decisions unique to each person.

• Distinctive attitude and evaluative language.

• Comparable dimensions: expectations, obstacles, reactions, benefits, regrets, motivation, change, advice, relationships or outcomes.

• Paraphrasable evidence: the question wording should not merely repeat the profile’s key words.

Tone / voice

Natural, differentiated voices. First person works especially well, but third-person mini-profiles are also valid. Each person should sound plausible without exaggerated dialect or caricature.

Typical ending

• Each profile should end on its own meaningful state: outcome, current view, consequence, reflection or next step.

• Avoid giving all four profiles the same rhetorical ending or identical 'now I...' structure.

Avoid

• Four versions of the same story with names changed.

• One profile containing nearly all the strong examinable material.

• Obvious lexical matching between question and answer.

• Artificially forcing every profile through the same paragraph template.

• Voices differentiated only by superficial hobbies or demographic labels.

• Contradictory or insufficient evidence for the matching questions.

References used

7.1 — Four people describe how they turned their hobbies into new careers. Excellent comparison set: common transformation theme, but different triggers, obstacles, confidence levels, commercial decisions and skill development.

7.6 — What was university like for you?. Strong differentiated voices with overlapping dimensions—money, study, career, identity and mistakes—distributed in different combinations.

7.5 — Four rising stars. Demonstrates third-person/quoted mini-profiles with distinct developmental paths, setbacks, expectations and chance opportunities.

ENGINE NOTE  Part 7 requires this family because the content itself must be architected for cross-profile matching. The Content Brief should explicitly plan comparable dimensions and differentiation before the structural prompt writes the ten questions.



8. Pilot validation checklist

These cards are now written and referenced. The remaining step is practical validation with new DRALO content—not further theoretical expansion.

• Generate at least two new Content Briefs from each Style Card using different Topic Bank entries.

• Check whether the generated briefs are recognisably different across Style Cards even when topics are similar.

• Check whether each card creates enough substance without forcing invented facts.

• Check whether Parts 1–3 can compress the style naturally without sounding formulaic.

• Check whether Parts 5–6 retain strong paragraph progression and support the construct of the Part.

• For SC-06, verify that four profiles are differentiated yet comparable and that matching questions can be paraphrased without keyword dependence.

• Record Keep / Change / Add / Remove / Do not feedback and revise only where the pilot exposes a repeatable problem.
