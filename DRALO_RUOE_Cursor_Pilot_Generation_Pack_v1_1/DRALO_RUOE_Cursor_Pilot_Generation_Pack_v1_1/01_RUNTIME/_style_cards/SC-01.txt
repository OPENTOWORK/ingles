SC-01  ·  STYLE CARD

Curiosity & Explanatory Article

STATUSReady for pilot validation

LANGUAGEBritish English

LEVELCEFR B2

What content it suits

Questions, phenomena, behaviours, natural/scientific curiosities and everyday mechanisms that can be explained clearly without becoming academic.

Compatible Parts

Parts 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6.

Typical opening

• Begin with a familiar observation, surprising fact, accessible question or apparent contradiction.

• Create an immediate knowledge gap: the reader should understand what is interesting before receiving the explanation.

• Avoid opening with a dry definition unless the definition itself is surprising or necessary.

Content progression

• Move from the familiar phenomenon to the underlying explanation.

• Introduce one or more concrete examples, mechanisms, observations or pieces of evidence.

• Where useful, present an initial assumption and then refine, complicate or correct it.

• Build paragraphs so each one answers a new part of the reader’s implicit question rather than restating the same idea.

• For short Parts 1–3, compress this into a miniature arc: hook → explanation → one concrete development → meaningful closing point.

What gives it real substance

• A precise phenomenon rather than a broad theme.

• Specific mechanisms, causes, consequences or contrasts.

• Concrete examples that make the explanation memorable.

• Careful factuality: plausible general explanations are acceptable, but invented studies, institutions, statistics or quotations are not.

Tone / voice

Accessible, intelligent and curious. Neutral-to-engaged rather than textbook-like. The writer sounds interested in explaining something to a general reader, not lecturing them.

Typical ending

• Close by resolving the initial curiosity, revealing a wider implication, or showing why the phenomenon matters.

• An effective final sentence may slightly broaden the perspective, but should not become generic advice or a moral.

Avoid

• Encyclopaedia-style lists of facts with no progression.

• Overloading the text with technical terminology.

• Generic openings such as 'Nowadays, X is very important'.

• Unsupported claims presented as research findings.

• Repeating the same explanatory point in different wording.

• Turning an explanation into self-help.

References used

5.2 — Birth order – does it make any difference?. Strong model of question-led explanation, competing ideas, evidence and a conclusion that remains intellectually open rather than simplistic.

2.2 — Amazing fungi!. Compact curiosity-led explanation: familiar object → hidden reality → striking example → current area of interest.

3.2 — The grass-eating shark. Uses a surprising natural phenomenon, competing explanations and concrete biological detail to sustain curiosity.

ENGINE NOTE  Use the card to shape the editorial arc only. Any statistics, studies or factual claims in a generated brief must be verifiable or deliberately framed as non-specific/plausible rather than invented.
