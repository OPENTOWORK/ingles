#### Texts A–D

##### A. Alex - protected focus periods

I work mainly on complex design and analysis tasks that require deep concentration, but constant interruptions used to make this difficult. Our team decided to introduce fixed hours when nobody could schedule meetings or send direct messages, so we could focus without distractions. Initially, it felt a bit strange to pause normal communication, and some quick questions had to wait until the blocks ended. However, I learnt to jot down thoughts and handle them later, which helped maintain my flow. While I appreciate these protected focus periods for allowing me to complete demanding work without switching tasks, I wouldn’t want them all day. For me, balancing uninterrupted time with moments to interact is key; I value clear boundaries that respect concentration but without total isolation.

##### B. Beth - flexible scheduling

Having strict start times for work was a source of daily stress, especially since I often have personal responsibilities around the same hours. When management offered the chance to choose start and finish times within a certain window, it made a huge difference. I still stick to agreed hours overall, but deciding exactly when to begin and end gives me much more control. Of course, this flexibility means we must communicate handovers carefully to avoid confusion with colleagues on different schedules. We use shared status notes for this, making sure the next person knows what’s happening. Ultimately, I find managing my own workday more important than reducing the number of hours worked. Predictability combined with choice has become my top priority.

##### C. Charlie - mentoring a new colleague

When I was asked to mentor a new team member, my first thought was that it would add extra workload and slow me down. Mentoring requires patience because explaining what’s second nature to me means reflecting on procedures I’d never questioned before. Over time, though, I realised I was helping someone become independent, which felt rewarding beyond expectations. I also found that teaching tasks helped me see them more clearly myself and identify areas for my own improvement. Even though mentoring occasionally means repeating myself and takes time away from my own tasks, I appreciate the sense of usefulness it brings and the quiet pride in contributing to someone else’s growth.

##### D. Dana - problem-solving meetings

Our team used to deal with recurring issues individually, which meant the same problems were raised repeatedly without real solutions. We introduced short, regular problem-solving sessions where one member presents an issue and everyone affected discusses possible fixes. We end each meeting by agreeing on one practical action to follow up. Initially, it was a challenge to keep these discussions efficient and avoid going over the same points. Some meetings felt repetitive when no clear decisions were made. Nonetheless, the process encouraged shared responsibility and a better understanding of challenges across the team. While I appreciate this collaborative approach, I’m impatient with meetings that don’t lead to concrete outcomes.
