FULL MONOLOGUE SCRIPT
================================================================================

Hello. My name's Elena, and for the past four years I've been a volunteer with
the Lakes Peak Mountain Rescue Team. People often imagine we're out climbing
every weekend for fun, but most of what we do is training — because when a call
comes in, there is no time to hesitate.

Before anyone is accepted, they have to pass an annual fitness test. It's not
about being the fastest runner in the group; the instructors want to see that
you can keep going uphill with a heavy pack for several hours without becoming
a liability to the rest of the squad. After that, there's a basic background
check, and only then do you join the trainee programme.

The programme starts in the classroom. New volunteers learn navigation — map
reading, compass work, and how to describe a location accurately when you're
speaking to the police or the ambulance service. I still remember my first night
exercise in thick cloud on the lower slopes. I thought I knew the path, but
without proper navigation training I would simply have walked in circles.

Every member carries a whistle, which sounds obvious, but in wind you can't
rely on shouting. One short blast means "Where are you?" and two means "Come to
me." We drill those signals until they're automatic, because on a ridge you may
not be able to see the person ten metres ahead of you.

The initial course also includes an accredited first aid qualification. You're
taught to treat hypothermia, stabilise a leg injury, and keep someone calm while
you wait for professional paramedics. The certificate has to be renewed, so we
run refreshers every spring.

Outdoor kit has to be fully waterproof — not shower-resistant, not "good enough
for town," but capable of keeping you dry in horizontal rain above the treeline.
The team publishes a kit list, and leaders check your bag before major
exercises. Cheap gloves that soak through in twenty minutes are not acceptable.

When someone is seriously injured on a remote ridge, we request a helicopter
from the national search service. The pilot cannot land on a narrow ledge, so
we prepare a landing site lower down and carry the casualty on a stretcher to
meet the aircraft. Those exercises are exhausting, but they teach you how much
coordination a real rescue requires.

All team leaders are unpaid and work on a voluntary basis. Employers are
generally supportive because they know the skills — leadership, communication,
staying calm under pressure — transfer back to the workplace. Even so, balancing
shift work with sudden call-outs at two in the morning is not easy.

Ropes are used most often on steep ground where a slip would have serious
consequences. You learn to set up anchors, belay a colleague, and descend
safely with a load. It's repetitive practice on the same rocky outcrop month
after month until the procedures feel boring — and that's exactly the point.

Call-outs frequently happen in poor visibility. Fog, sleet, and darkness combine
to turn a familiar path into something disorientating. GPS helps, but batteries
fail and screens crack, so we still teach old-fashioned techniques with map and
compass.

Many recruits tell me the training gave them greater confidence in the mountains.
Not arrogance — you never stop respecting the weather — but a quiet belief that
if something went wrong, you would know what to do first, who to contact, and
how to keep yourself and others safe until help arrives.

  Single common noun; fits "a ___ test" grammatically.

  She says "New volunteers learn navigation — map reading, compass work…"
  The examples clarify the head noun; "map reading" alone would ignore compass

  Personal signalling device when shouting fails in wind. Radios are not the

  Accredited qualification on the initial course, distinct from navigation or
  fitness modules. Standard two-word B2 phrase.

  Kit must be "fully waterproof," not merely shower-resistant. Adjective
  completes "fully ___" naturally.

14 — helicopter
  Requested from the national search service for remote ridge evacuations.

15 — voluntary
  Leaders are unpaid and work on a "voluntary basis." Required adjective form.

  Ropes used on "steep ground" where falls have serious consequences.

17 — visibility
  Introduced as "poor visibility" before fog and sleet examples. "Fog" is an
  instance, not the category named first in the audio.
